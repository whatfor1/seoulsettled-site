You wake up with a fever, your Korean is limited to “kamsahamnida”, and Google Maps shows forty places that all say “의원” or “병원”. Which door do you walk through? Here is how to see a doctor in Seoul as a foreigner.

## Short version

For a cold, a stomach bug or a minor ache, walk into a neighborhood clinic. For anything serious, or if you want English-speaking staff, go to a big hospital’s international health center on a weekday. For an emergency, call 119.

## Clinic or hospital: which one do you need?

The Working Holiday Info Center, run by Korea’s foreign ministry, [describes two main types](https://whic.mofa.go.kr/contents.do?contentsNo=46&menuNo=108) of medical institutions. Primary care means local clinics, public health centers and small hospitals that treat common illnesses such as colds, flu and digestive problems. General hospitals, including specialized and university hospitals, handle more serious conditions and detailed examinations.

Jun’s tip: in Korea, the doctor you see is chosen by the body part, not by a single family doctor. A clinic with “이비인후과” on the sign is ear, nose and throat, “내과” is internal medicine, “정형외과” is orthopedics. Searching a Korean map app for the department name near you finds the right clinic faster than typing “doctor”.

Two other options exist. The same page notes that almost every neighborhood has an Oriental medicine clinic (acupuncture, cupping), and that public health centers are state-run and cost much less than private institutions for things like vaccinations and chronic-disease care.

## How to find a clinic that can help you in English

If you are searching for an English-friendly clinic in Seoul, start in your map app. Search the department name, then open the listing and check for English on the sign or the description. If you are unsure, walk in and show a typed symptom note on your phone. Naver Map is the one that actually finds Korean addresses and department names reliably — see my [Naver Map setup guide](/naver-map-english-guide-foreigners/) if you haven't switched it to English yet.

If the language gap worries you, use the big hospitals. The Korea Tourism Organization notes that [many of Korea’s large hospitals run an International Health Care Center](https://english.visitkorea.or.kr/svc/contents/infoHtmlView.do?vcontsId=140042) with interpreters who help from reservation to follow-up. Its list for Seoul includes Seoul Asan Medical Center, Severance Hospital, Samsung Medical Center, Seoul National University Hospital, Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital, Gangnam Severance Hospital, Korea University Medical Center (Anam) and Kyung Hee University Medical Center. The page lists English inquiry lines and exact hours — Seoul Asan Medical Center and Severance Hospital both run their center from 08:30 to 17:30 on weekdays, a window of about 9 hours, closed on weekends and public holidays, so call on a weekday morning.

- Your ARC (or passport if you're still inside the 90 days you get to register for one, or a short-term visitor), since clinics register you as a patient with ID

- Your health insurance card or your travel insurance details

- A credit or debit card

- A short note on your phone with your symptoms, how long they have lasted, allergies and any medicines you take

If you have lived here for more than 6 months, the same foreign-ministry page says you are required to enroll in the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS). Questions about enrollment or exemption go to NHIS at 1577-1000, and for foreign-language help press 7 or call 033-811-2000. My earlier guide on [getting your ARC in Korea](/how-to-get-arc-korea/) covers the card you will show at the desk, and my [guide to NHIS costs](/korea-national-health-insurance-foreigners-cost/) covers what you'll owe once you're enrolled.

## What a visit actually costs

There's no single sticker price, because your share of the bill depends on the type of care and the type of facility. NHIS's own page on [insurance benefits](https://www.nhis.or.kr/english/wbheaa02600m01.do) lays out the co-payment (your share after NHIS pays the rest) for outpatient visits: 30% of the covered cost at a neighborhood clinic, 40% at a hospital, 45-50% at a general hospital depending on whether it's in a city or a rural district, and 60% at a tertiary or upper-level general hospital. Filling a prescription at the pharmacy costs another 30% co-payment. That scale is deliberate — NHIS designed it to push routine visits toward clinics instead of the big hospitals.

A general health checkup through NHIS is free of charge, and a handful of extra blood-pressure or diabetes tests get added depending on your age. The six national cancer screenings (stomach, colorectal, breast, cervical, liver and lung) are billed 90% to NHIS and 10% to you, except colorectal and cervical screening, which have no co-payment at all.

None of this applies if you don't have NHIS yet. Without a card, you're billed the full, non-discounted rate the clinic or hospital charges — still fine for a cold, but worth knowing before you assume a visit will be cheap.

Jun’s tip: write the symptom note in two lines, English on top and a translation underneath. A written note saves you from explaining symptoms out loud.

## What happens at the clinic, step by step

1. Hand your ID to the front desk and fill in the short registration form.

2. Wait. Smaller clinics are often walk-in; larger hospitals and specialist departments are more likely to use appointments, so check before you go.

3. See the doctor, show your note, and ask them to write the diagnosis down if you want to look it up later.

4. Pay at the desk, then take the prescription to a pharmacy. Pharmacies are marked 약국.

Jun’s tip: prescriptions in Korea are filled at pharmacies, not at the clinic window. Look for a 약국 sign near the clinic and hand over the paper.

## Emergencies and after-hours help

The Korea Tourism Organization page lists these numbers: 112 for police, 119 for fire and medical emergencies, and 1339 for infectious disease information. The foreign ministry page adds that 129 is the public health and welfare call center, and that 1339 is the emergency medical information line. For travel help and interpretation that is not an emergency, the 1330 Korea Travel Hotline offers English among its languages.

My rule: if it is life-threatening, call 119 and do not wait. If you are only unsure which place is open at 10 p.m., call 1339 or 1330 and ask.

A 119 ambulance for a genuine emergency is free nationwide, regardless of how far it drives or how many patients it carries, according to Korea's [Easy Law portal page on emergency medical costs](https://easylaw.go.kr/CSP/CnpClsMain.laf?popMenu=ov&csmSeq=906&ccfNo=2&cciNo=4&cnpClsNo=1). That free ride only covers genuine emergencies, though. A private or hospital-run ambulance used for a non-urgent transfer between facilities is billed separately: a standard ambulance runs 40,000 won for the first 10 km (26,600 won if it's run by a non-profit), plus 1,500 won per additional kilometer, and a specially-equipped ambulance starts at 95,500 won for the first 10 km plus 2,300 won per kilometer after that. All of those rates go up 20% on weeknights from 6 p.m. to 9 a.m., on Saturdays and on public holidays, and the ambulance can charge 6,000 won for every 10 minutes it waits once it's been at the hospital more than 30 minutes.

## If something goes wrong

**The hospital or clinic doesn't have your insurance on file, or your ARC hasn't arrived yet.** You can still be treated — you'll just be billed the full rate instead of the NHIS co-payment rate described above. Sort out your ARC and NHIS enrollment as soon as you can; my [ARC guide](/how-to-get-arc-korea/) and [NHIS cost guide](/korea-national-health-insurance-foreigners-cost/) cover both.

**A hospital refuses to treat you in an emergency.** Under the Emergency Medical Service Act, emergency staff cannot refuse or avoid treating an emergency patient without a valid reason, per the same Easy Law portal. What if they do it anyway? The law treats it as serious: a first violation costs the provider's staff a 2 months suspension of their license, a second violation 3 months, and a third gets the license revoked outright. If a doctor turns you away from an ER for no stated reason, that's not a gray area — report it.

**The bill looks wrong.** The same government page points you to the Health Insurance Review & Assessment Service (HIRA) site, under "조회·신청 → 진료비 확인" (fee inquiry/confirmation), to check whether what a hospital charged you was appropriate. If it's too late to use that after you've already paid, HIRA can still review it and refund the overcharge.

**You need an English diagnosis or certificate for an insurance claim back home.** There's no fixed national price for these certificates — HIRA's own non-covered-fee disclosures confirm each hospital sets its own rate, so what if the hospital you're at charges more than you expected? Ask the front desk for the certificate fee before they print it, not after.
