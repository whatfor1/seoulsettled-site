You wake up with a fever, your Korean is limited to “kamsahamnida”, and Google Maps shows forty places that all say “의원” or “병원”. Which door do you walk through? Here is how to see a doctor in Seoul as a foreigner.

## Short version

For a cold, a stomach bug or a minor ache, walk into a neighborhood clinic. For anything serious, or if you want English-speaking staff, go to a big hospital’s international health center on a weekday. For an emergency, call 119.

## Clinic or hospital: which one do you need?

The Working Holiday Info Center, run by Korea’s foreign ministry, [describes two main types](https://whic.mofa.go.kr/contents.do?contentsNo=46&menuNo=108) of medical institutions. Primary care means local clinics, public health centers and small hospitals that treat common illnesses such as colds, flu and digestive problems. General hospitals, including specialized and university hospitals, handle more serious conditions and detailed examinations.

Jun’s tip: in Korea, the doctor you see is chosen by the body part, not by a single family doctor. A clinic with “이비인후과” on the sign is ear, nose and throat, “내과” is internal medicine, “정형외과” is orthopedics. Searching a Korean map app for the department name near you finds the right clinic faster than typing “doctor”.

Two other options exist. The same page notes that almost every neighborhood has an Oriental medicine clinic (acupuncture, cupping), and that public health centers are state-run and cost much less than private institutions for things like vaccinations and chronic-disease care.

## How to find a clinic that can help you in English

If you are searching for an English-friendly clinic in Seoul, start in your map app. Search the department name, then open the listing and check for English on the sign or the description. If you are unsure, walk in and show a typed symptom note on your phone.

If the language gap worries you, use the big hospitals. The Korea Tourism Organization notes that [many of Korea’s large hospitals run an International Health Care Center](https://english.visitkorea.or.kr/svc/contents/infoHtmlView.do?vcontsId=140042) with interpreters who help from reservation to follow-up. Its list for Seoul includes Seoul Asan Medical Center, Severance Hospital, Samsung Medical Center, Seoul National University Hospital, Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital, Gangnam Severance Hospital, Korea University Medical Center (Anam) and Kyung Hee University Medical Center. The page lists English inquiry lines and says the centers operate Monday to Friday, closed on weekends and public holidays, so call on a weekday morning.

- Your ARC (or passport if you are a short-term visitor), since clinics register you as a patient with ID

- Your health insurance card or your travel insurance details

- A credit or debit card

- A short note on your phone with your symptoms, how long they have lasted, allergies and any medicines you take

If you have lived here for more than six months, the same foreign-ministry page says you are required to enroll in the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS). Questions about enrollment or exemption go to NHIS at 1577-1000, and for foreign-language help press 7 or call 033-811-2000. My earlier guide on [getting your ARC in Korea](/how-to-get-arc-korea/) covers the card you will show at the desk.

Jun’s tip: write the symptom note in two lines, English on top and a translation underneath. A written note saves you from explaining symptoms out loud.

## What happens at the clinic, step by step

1. Hand your ID to the front desk and fill in the short registration form.

2. Wait. Smaller clinics are often walk-in; larger hospitals and specialist departments are more likely to use appointments, so check before you go.

3. See the doctor, show your note, and ask them to write the diagnosis down if you want to look it up later.

4. Pay at the desk, then take the prescription to a pharmacy. Pharmacies are marked 약국.

Jun’s tip: prescriptions in Korea are filled at pharmacies, not at the clinic window. Look for a 약국 sign near the clinic and hand over the paper.

## Emergencies and after-hours help

The Korea Tourism Organization page lists these numbers: 112 for police, 119 for fire and medical emergencies, and 1339 for infectious disease information. The foreign ministry page adds that 129 is the public health and welfare call center, and that 1339 is the emergency medical information line. For travel help and interpretation that is not an emergency, the 1330 Korea Travel Hotline offers English among its languages.

My rule: if it is life-threatening, call 119 and do not wait. If you are only unsure which place is open at 10 p.m., call 1339 or 1330 and ask.

## Quick checklist

- Save 119, 1339 and 1330 in your phone today

- Put your ARC, insurance card and a payment card in one pouch

- Keep a symptom note in English with a Korean translation

- Learn three department words: 내과, 이비인후과, 정형외과

- Check NHIS enrollment if you have been here six months
