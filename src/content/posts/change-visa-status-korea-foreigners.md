Our [extension guide](/extend-stay-period-korea-visa-extension-hikorea/) covers keeping the status you already have past its expiry date. This one covers something legally different: swapping that status for a different one entirely — D-2 student to E-7 specialized worker, D-4 language track to D-2 degree track, D-10 job-seeker to E-7, or a marriage that opens the door to F-6. Korea's immigration law treats these as two separate permissions with two separate fee schedules, and mixing them up on an application is one of the easier ways to waste a HiKorea appointment.

## Extension keeps your status. This changes it.

[Article 24 of the Immigration Control Act](https://www.law.go.kr/LSW/lsLawLinkInfo.do?lsJoLnkSeq=1000822779&chrClsCd=010202) requires anyone who wants to carry out activities under a different status of stay to get the Minister of Justice's approval for a 체류자격 변경허가 (status-change permit) before they start. That's a different filing from the 체류기간 연장허가 our other guide walks through: extension pushes your existing status's expiry date out; a status change swaps the category itself, and the practical difference matters — you cannot legally start the new job, the new degree program, or whatever activity the new status covers until the change is approved. Taking up activity under a status you don't yet hold is its own violation: [Article 94, item 12](https://www.law.go.kr/LSW/lsLinkProc.do?lsNm=%EC%B6%9C%EC%9E%85%EA%B5%AD%EA%B4%80%EB%A6%AC%EB%B2%95&joNo=009400&mode=11) sets the penalty for working or studying outside your current status without permission at **up to 3 years in prison or a 30,000,000 won fine** — the same ceiling the law sets for overstaying altogether.

## The one deadline the law actually names: 30 days

Most status changes don't carry a fixed statutory countdown the way extension does — you simply have to get approval before you start the new activity, whenever that is. There's one clear exception, and it's worth knowing by heart: under [Article 24, paragraph 2](https://www.law.go.kr/LSW/lsLawLinkInfo.do?lsJoLnkSeq=1000822779&chrClsCd=010202), if your need to change status comes from a change in personal circumstances — marriage, divorce, the end of a parent-child relationship, or similar status-affecting events listed under Article 31, paragraph 1 — you must file for the status-change permit within **30 days** of that change taking place. Graduating from a degree program that your current D-2 or D-4 status was tied to falls in the same practical category: the clock to sort out your next status starts the day the old basis for your stay ends.

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Confirm which category you're actually filing: status change (switching categories) or extension (same category, later date) — they use different HiKorea application types</li>
  <li>If the trigger is marriage, divorce, or a similar personal-status change, file within 30 days of that change — this is the one deadline the law states as a fixed number</li>
  <li>Don't start the new job, program, or activity before approval — unauthorized status-outside activity carries its own penalty, separate from overstaying</li>
  <li>Check your specific status pair's document list on HiKorea before your appointment — the attachments required run status by status, not one generic list</li>
</ul>

## What it costs — and the one status that costs double

[The civil-service listing for registered foreigners changing status](https://www.gov.kr/portal/service/serviceInfo/PTR000050536) confirms the baseline: **100,000 won**, paid as a government revenue stamp (정부 수입인지), with the fee waived for Argentine nationals under 14 and for corporate-investor (D-8) status changes. The enforcement rule behind that fee sets one notable exception the other way: [출입국관리법 시행규칙 제72조](https://www.law.go.kr/LSW/lsLawLinkInfo.do?chrClsCd=010202&lsJoLnkSeq=1000817401) doubles the fee to **200,000 won** specifically when the status you're changing into is F-5 permanent residency — confirmed on the same statute by the Ministry of Government Legislation's plain-language [easylaw.go.kr guide to visa changes](https://easylaw.go.kr/CSP/CnpClsMain.laf?popMenu=ov&csmSeq=1703&ccfNo=1&cciNo=4&cnpClsNo=2), which states the fee directly as "일반: 10만원 / 영주권(F-5) 소유자: 20만원." File through HiKorea's electronic application rather than in person and the same fee-reduction rule our extension guide found applies a **20%** discount to status-change filings as well, the same as it does for extension and re-entry permits.

If you're on a short-term stay of 90 days or less rather than already ARC-registered — a C-3 tourist entry, for instance — [the separate short-term-stayer service listing](https://www.gov.kr/portal/service/serviceInfo/PTR000050535) confirms the same 100,000 won fee and the same two exemptions apply to you too, with one extra document requirement: a 3×4cm color photo, since you won't have an ARC photo already on file the way a registered foreigner does.

## Documents and processing time

Every status-change application shares a common core: the application form (서식 제34호), your passport or foreigner entry permit, and your Alien Registration Card for anyone already registered. Beyond that, the gov.kr listing is direct about the limit of a single generic checklist: additional attachments are set "체류자격별로" — by status category — under 출입국관리법 시행규칙 별표 5의2, so a D-2-to-E-7 change and a D-4-to-D-2 change don't require the same supporting documents. Look up your specific pair on HiKorea's own per-status guide manuals (하이코리아 → 정보광장 → 체류자격별 안내매뉴얼) before you book an appointment, rather than assuming your friend's document list matches yours.

Processing time is the other place this differs sharply from extension, which at least publishes a 15–60 day range. For status change, the gov.kr listing states processing time plainly as **미정** — undetermined — adding only that it "통상 3주~3개월가량 소요" (typically takes about 3 weeks to 3 months). That's a wide window, and it's wide because the review depends on the specific status you're moving into, not a fixed administrative clock the way extension's is.

## When it goes wrong

**Your application gets denied.** The immigration office has to issue a written denial, and under current practice that notice follows the same administrative framework as a denied extension — including a departure deadline where one applies. Read whatever deadline is printed on your specific notice and ask the issuing office directly which of your options (reapplying with different documents, appealing, or arranging departure) actually fits your case, rather than guessing from this guide or anyone else's.

**You're missing a status-specific document.** Since the attachment list runs by status category rather than one master list, a rejected application for a missing document is common and usually fixable the same day — the branch will tell you exactly which attachment is missing, and most applicants can bring it back within the processing window rather than restarting the whole filing.

**You're about to blow the 30-day personal-status-change deadline.** If your status needs to change because of marriage, divorce, or a similar event and the 30-day window from Article 24(2) is closing, go to your immigration office before day 30, not after, even without every document ready. Showing up before the deadline with an incomplete application is a materially different position than filing late.

**Your employer wants you to start before approval comes through.** Don't. Taking up the new status's activity — working the new job, enrolling in the new program — before the Minister of Justice approves the change exposes you to the unauthorized-activity penalty under Article 94, item 12, separate from and in addition to whatever happens to the pending application itself. If an employer is pushing you to start early, that pressure is on them to manage, not a risk worth taking on your own ARC.

**You're not sure whether to file for extension or status change at all.** If your activities under your current visa aren't actually changing — same job, same school, just more time — you want extension, not status change. If what you'll be doing changes, even within the same broad visa family, you need a status change. When genuinely unsure which filing applies to your situation, call 외국인종합안내센터 at **1345** before you book a HiKorea appointment for the wrong form.
