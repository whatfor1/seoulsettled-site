Search "can foreigners buy Korean stocks" and a lot of what comes up is outdated. Until late 2023, any foreign investor had to pre-register with Korea's financial regulator and get an investment registration number before buying a single share. That system is gone. What's left is simpler on paper but still has real friction points — a residency cutoff that catches new arrivals, a document list that trips people up, and a tax bill that nobody emails you about. Here's what actually opening and holding a Korean brokerage account involves now.

## The pre-registration system is gone — here's what replaced it

Korea's Financial Services Commission abolished the foreign investor pre-registration system effective **December 14, 2023**. Before that date, a foreigner needed to register with the Financial Supervisory Service and receive an Investment Registration Certificate (IRC) before a broker would let them buy listed shares. The FSC's own notice on the change confirms what replaced it: individual foreign investors are now identified by [passport number alone](https://www.fsc.go.kr/no040101?cnId=2017), with corporate investors identified by their LEI (Legal Entity Identifier) instead. A companion FSC policy page spells out the practical effect — the broker now runs its own identity-verification process and opens the account directly, [with no separate pre-registration step](https://www.fsc.go.kr/po010101/79326) in between. If you already held an old IRC number, it still works; you just don't need a new one.

That's a genuine simplification. It doesn't mean anyone can walk in and open an account the same day they land, though — the residency rule below still applies.

## Can you even open one: the 6-month line

Kiwoom Securities' own account-opening page for foreigners states the eligibility rule plainly: **non-resident foreigners cannot open a brokerage account at all**, and no one — not a spouse, not a coworker — can open one by proxy on a foreigner's behalf. Resident foreigners need one of two things, per [Kiwoom's page](https://www.kiwoom.com/h/banking/sales/VSalesFoAcntOpenView): an Alien Registration Card (ARC) issued **6 months or more** ago, or a domestic residence report of the same age if you're an overseas Korean. Either way you also need proof of your Korean address and a copy of your own bank passbook.

If your ARC is newer than 6 months, you're not automatically shut out — you just need to bring more. The same page lists the fallback documents: a certificate of employment, an entry/exit certificate showing you haven't left Korea for 6 months straight, or a stay-permit application receipt. In practice, this means someone who just landed and registered their [ARC within the standard 90 days](/how-to-get-arc-korea/) of entry — a card that itself costs a flat 35,000 won — can usually still open an account, but should expect to produce one of those extra papers rather than just the card itself.

Mirae Asset Securities' own foreigner account information corroborates the non-resident exclusion from the other side: its page for foreign customers also requires an ARC or domestic residence report, with no path at all for someone who hasn't established residency. If you've moved since your ARC was issued, report the new address to your local 주민센터 within 15 days — an address on file that doesn't match your current proof of residence is one more thing that can stall the application.

## What the application actually involves

Once you clear the residency bar, the mechanics are close to opening any other account here. You need a Korean bank account already open — the broker links your trading account to it for deposits, withdrawals and dividend payouts — so if you haven't done that yet, start with [opening a Korean bank account](/open-bank-account-korea-foreigner/) first; the passbook copy Kiwoom asks for comes from that same account. Make sure that account isn't still a restricted one — missing the proof-of-purpose document on the bank visit caps it at 3,000,000 won a day at the counter and 1,000,000 won through ATMs or apps, which is worth clearing before you start funding a brokerage account on top of it. Some banks also waive their own transfer fees once a 500,000 won salary deposit has landed in the account for the month, which matters once dividends start arriving there too.

You'll also need a Korean phone number registered in your own name, for the same identity-verification reasons covered in our [guide to Korean identity verification](/korea-identity-verification-pass-app-foreigners/) — brokers run the same real-name check that banking apps and PASS do. A bank-issued financial certificate (금융인증서), if you already set one up, is valid for 3 years and renewable from 90 days before it expires — a lot less upkeep than the old certificate it replaced, which needed renewing every 1 year — and several brokers accept it as a login method once your account exists.

The name-matching rule that trips people up on credit cards trips them up here too: your name has to read identically, spacing included, across your passport, ARC, bank account and the brokerage application. Our [credit card guide](/credit-card-korea-foreigners/) covers this exact failure mode in more detail, and the fix is the same — correct the record that's wrong, then resubmit, rather than trying a different broker.

**Branch or app — this one depends on who you ask.** Some information floating around online says foreigners can open a brokerage account the same non-face-to-face way Korean nationals do, entirely through an app. Mirae Asset's own foreigner-account page says otherwise for its own service: no non-face-to-face opening for foreigners, branch visit required. A third-party source claims the opposite for the same broker. Rather than guess which one is current, the honest answer is that this varies by broker and even by who you ask at the same broker — call the broker's own foreigner support line before you plan your trip, and don't assume either way until someone on the phone confirms it for the specific account you want.

## What it costs to actually hold Korean stocks

Opening the account is free at every major broker. Holding and trading isn't tax-free, and the two numbers that apply to almost every retail investor are worth knowing before your first trade, not after your first dividend payment:

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Dividend withholding: 15.4% combined (14% national plus 1.4% local surtax), deducted automatically before the dividend hits your account</li>
  <li>Securities transaction tax: 0.20% on every sale of KOSPI-listed shares, and 0.20% on KOSDAQ or K-OTC shares, deducted automatically by the broker — not something you calculate or file yourself</li>
</ul>

The dividend figure comes straight from the Income Tax Act. Article 129, Paragraph 1, Item 2 sets the withholding rate on ordinary dividend income at **14%** national tax — confirmed against the statute text itself via [casenote.kr's mirror of the law](https://casenote.kr/법령/소득세법/제129조) — and the standard 10%-of-national-tax local surtax (the same convention used for every other withholding figure on this site) adds another 1.4%, for the familiar 15.4% total. That 14% figure is the resident rate; Korea separately draws its tax-residency line at 183 days in the country, a different count from the 6-month brokerage-eligibility rule above, so don't assume the two match just because both sound like "about half a year."

The transaction-tax figure is less clean to source. Korea's Ministry of Economy and Finance amended the enforcement decree as part of its "2025 tax reform," and three independent financial-press outlets — Hankyung, Newsway and Taxtimes — all report the identical result: effective **January 1, 2026**, KOSPI-listed shares carry a 0.05% securities transaction tax plus a 0.15% agricultural and fishery special tax, for 0.20% combined, while KOSDAQ and K-OTC shares carry a flat 0.20% transaction tax with no special tax layered on top. I tried to confirm this against the primary enforcement-decree text on Korea's Easy Law portal directly and got repeated server errors on the page, so this figure is press-sourced rather than a direct statute quote — worth flagging, even though three outlets independently agree on the same number.

What I'm not printing here is a specific capital-gains threshold. Ordinary retail trading on KOSPI- and KOSDAQ-listed shares generally isn't taxed as a capital gain at all — Korea reserves that for "major shareholders" who cross an ownership threshold that has moved over time and that I couldn't confirm against a single current, fetchable primary source today. If your holding in any one company is large enough that this might apply to you, call the National Tax Service at 126 or talk to a tax advisor rather than trust a number pulled from a blog — this one is genuinely worth getting from someone who can see your specific position.

## Your cash is protected. Your stocks aren't.

This distinction matters and gets blurred constantly: the won sitting in your brokerage account as uninvested cash (투자자예탁금, "investor deposit") is protected up to **100,000,000 won** combined principal and interest per institution through Korea's deposit insurance system, KDIC — the same coverage limit and mechanism already in use on our bank account guide for KB's Global Star account, raised from the old 50,000,000 won ceiling in September 2025.

That protection stops the moment your cash becomes a share. If the stock you bought drops, KDIC does not reimburse the loss — it only protects the cash balance against the brokerage itself failing, not against the market moving against you. Treat these as two completely separate kinds of safety.

## When it goes wrong

**Your application gets rejected and nobody explains why.** The two most common reasons are a name mismatch across your passport, ARC and bank account, or an ARC that's under 6 months old without the fallback documents (certificate of employment, entry/exit record, or stay-permit receipt) attached. Ask the broker's foreigner support line which specific document or field failed before you resubmit — Kiwoom's line is 1544-2744; other brokers list similar numbers on their own foreigner pages.

**You assumed online account opening would work and it didn't.** Given the conflicting information on this point, call ahead before you plan a trip around either assumption. If a branch visit turns out to be required, bring the same document set listed above — ARC or residence report, proof of address, bank passbook copy — so the trip isn't wasted on a second round of paperwork.

**You're not sure if a tax situation applies to you — major shareholder status, or you're about to leave Korea.** Leaving Korea changes your tax residency and, with it, your withholding treatment on dividends and gains going forward; that's treaty-dependent and genuinely not something a general guide should guess at for you. For either situation, call NTS at 126 or your broker's call center and get an answer specific to your account, rather than applying a number you found online to your own case.

**You want to move investment proceeds out of Korea.** That's a normal foreign-currency transfer and runs into the same documentation rules as any other outbound transfer — see our [guide to sending money abroad](/send-money-abroad-korea-foreigners/) for the $50,000 ceiling that resets every 12 months and applies to foreign residents.

## Checklist before you apply

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Confirm your ARC or domestic residence report is 6+ months old — if not, bring a certificate of employment, entry/exit record, or stay-permit receipt instead</li>
  <li>Open a Korean bank account first; you'll need its passbook copy for the brokerage application</li>
  <li>Make sure your name matches exactly — spacing included — across your passport, ARC, phone plan and bank account</li>
  <li>Call the broker's foreigner support line to confirm branch vs. online opening before you plan a trip</li>
  <li>Budget for 15.4% dividend withholding and 0.20% securities transaction tax on every sale — both are deducted automatically</li>
  <li>Remember KDIC's 100,000,000 won protection covers your cash balance only, not the value of shares you hold</li>
</ul>
