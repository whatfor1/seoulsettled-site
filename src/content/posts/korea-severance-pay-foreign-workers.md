Thirty days' average wage for every year you worked — that is the legal floor for severance pay (퇴직금) in Korea, and your passport has nothing to do with whether you're owed it. The Labor Standards Act and the Employee Retirement Benefit Security Act apply to every employment relationship in the country, Korean or foreign, as long as you worked continuously for at least a year. What trips people up isn't eligibility; it's the calculation, the 14-day payment clock, and what to do when an employer just doesn't pay.

## Who actually qualifies

Two conditions, and nationality is not one of them, per the [easylaw.go.kr summary of continuous-service rules](https://easylaw.go.kr/CSP/CnpClsMain.laf?popMenu=ov&csmSeq=999&ccfNo=3&cciNo=1&cnpClsNo=2):

- **At least 1 year of continuous service** with the same employer, counted from your contract start date to your last day.
- **An average of 15 or more scheduled hours per week**, measured over any 4-week period. Fall under that and your employer has no legal obligation to pay severance — this is the same rule that excludes very-part-time workers from paid leave and employment insurance.

Continuous service survives things you'd expect it to: approved leave (even unpaid), and back-to-back renewals of the same fixed-term contract. It does **not** survive a genuine break in employment — true single-day gig work with no expectation of renewal doesn't accumulate, and some employers exclude self-initiated personal leave if that's written into company policy. If your contract was renewed two or three times without a real gap, the renewals count as one continuous period, not separate one-year clocks that reset.

## How much you're owed — the 30-day formula

The baseline, under [근로자퇴직급여보장법 제8조 제1항](https://easylaw.go.kr/CSP/CnpClsMain.laf?popMenu=ov&csmSeq=514&ccfNo=6&cciNo=2&cnpClsNo=2), is:

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Find your "average wage" (평균임금): total wages paid in your last 3 months of work, divided by the actual number of calendar days in that 3-month span (not working days — every day counts).</li>
  <li>Multiply that daily average wage by 30.</li>
  <li>Multiply the result by your total years of continuous service (part-years count proportionally — 2.5 years pays 2.5 times the 30-day amount).</li>
</ul>

This isn't a guideline figure — it's defined exactly this way in [근로기준법 제2조](https://easylaw.go.kr/CSP/CnpClsMain.laf?popMenu=ov&csmSeq=1694&ccfNo=1&cciNo=2&cnpClsNo=1), which also sets a floor: if the 3-month average calculation comes out lower than your normal contracted wage (통상임금), the employer has to use the higher number instead. In practice, for someone on a flat monthly salary with no bonus swings, "30 days of average wage" lands close to one month's pay per year worked — but overtime, bonuses, or a raise partway through those 3 months will move the number, which is exactly why the law specifies the calculation rather than leaving it to "roughly one month."

## When it has to be paid

Your employer must pay severance **within 14 days** of your last working day, under [근로자퇴직급여보장법 제9조 제1항](https://easylaw.go.kr/CSP/CnpClsMain.laf?popMenu=ov&csmSeq=514&ccfNo=6&cciNo=2&cnpClsNo=2). That deadline can only be extended by mutual agreement between you and the employer — not unilaterally by the employer because payroll is "busy" or accounting is "catching up."

Miss that 14-day window without an agreement, and the employer owes **20% annual interest** on the unpaid amount, calculated from day 15 until the day it's actually paid. You also have **3 years** from your last day to make the claim at all — after that, the right to collect lapses under the same law.

## If you're on an E-9 or H-2 visa: the departure insurance route

Employers of E-9 (non-professional employment) and H-2 (working visit) visa holders don't pay severance directly out of pocket on departure — they're required to enroll you in **departure guarantee insurance (출국만기보험)** or an equivalent trust, within **15 days** of your contract taking effect (your entry date for a first-time E-9 arrival, or your contract start date for an H-2 worker or workplace changer).

The mechanics, confirmed on [easylaw.go.kr's foreign-worker employment guide](https://www.easylaw.go.kr/CSP/CnpClsMainBtr.laf?csmSeq=2042&ccfNo=3&cciNo=1&cnpClsNo=4) and its [departure-procedures page](https://www.easylaw.go.kr/CSP/CnpClsMainBtr.laf?popMenu=ov&csmSeq=2042&ccfNo=4&cciNo=2&cnpClsNo=1):

- The employer's premium is **8.3% of your monthly ordinary wage** — roughly the same ratio as one month's pay per 12 months worked, which is how the insurance is designed to approximate the statutory formula above.
- You can claim the payout once you've worked **1 year or more**, and the insurer pays out **within 14 days of your actual departure** from Korea (or within 14 days of your application date, if you apply after leaving).
- If the insurance payout comes in **lower** than what the 30-day formula above says you're owed, your employer has to pay you the difference directly — the insurance doesn't cap what you're legally entitled to, it just funds most of it in advance.
- You generally need to apply for the payout between **1 month and 7 days before** your scheduled departure date, and unclaimed insurance money also expires after **3 years**.

If you're on any other visa type — E-7, D-10, F-series, whatever brought you here — none of the insurance machinery applies to you. You're simply owed severance the standard way: direct payment from your employer, within 14 days, using the 30-day formula.

## When it goes wrong

**If your employer refuses to pay, or goes silent after you leave.** This is unpaid wage theft under Korean labor law, not a civil dispute you have to sort out alone. File a 진정 (complaint) with your local Employment and Labor office — in person, or by calling the labor counseling line at **1350** — within 1 year of the day after you left the job, to stay safely inside the filing window. A labor inspector investigates and, once they confirm the unpaid amount, issues documentation you can use to pursue payment, including small claims court if it comes to that.

**If the company is bankrupt or can't pay at all.** Korea has a government-backed fund for exactly this: **간이대지급금** (simplified wage claim guarantee payment), run through the Korea Workers' Compensation and Welfare Service (근로복지공단). It pays unpaid wages and severance on the employer's behalf, combined, up to **10,000,000 KRW** total. You apply after your labor-office complaint confirms the unpaid amount, through [근로복지공단's total service portal](https://total.comwel.or.kr) or in person at a regional branch.

**If your departure insurance payout looks short and your employer won't cover the gap.** Request a 평균임금 calculation breakdown from your employer in writing first — a wrong 3-month wage figure is the most common cause of a shortfall, not fraud. If they still won't pay the difference, that's the same wage-theft complaint process above; the insurance company isn't who you'd dispute it with.

**If your application to the labor office is rejected on a paperwork technicality**, or your 간이대지급금 request is denied for missing documentation, ask the caseworker exactly which document was wrong before resubmitting — a denied claim can usually be refiled once the gap is fixed, but you still need to stay inside the filing deadlines above. And if you miss the 3-year statute of limitations entirely, the claim is gone for good; there's no late exception written into the law for that one.

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Confirmed 1+ year of continuous service with the same employer</li>
  <li>Confirmed average 15+ scheduled hours per week over the last 4 weeks</li>
  <li>Calculated average wage: last 3 months' total pay ÷ calendar days in that span</li>
  <li>Multiplied by 30, then by years of service, to get the minimum owed</li>
  <li>Checked the 14-day payment deadline from your last working day</li>
  <li>If on E-9/H-2: confirmed departure insurance enrollment and applied 1 month to 7 days before leaving</li>
  <li>Saved payslips and your contract — you'll need them if you have to file a complaint</li>
</ul>
