Our [guide to changing visa status](/change-visa-status-korea-foreigners/) and the [D-10 job-seeking visa guide](/d10-job-seeking-visa-korea-foreigners/) both point toward E-7 as the destination once you land a job offer, without explaining what actually has to be true about that job offer first. E-7 (특정활동, "designated activity") isn't one visa with one set of rules — it's a list of 94 occupations the Ministry of Justice has individually approved for foreign hires, and each one carries its own mix of education, experience, salary floor and company-side conditions. Before you or your employer files anything, the job title on your contract has to match one of those 94 entries.

## The four E-7 categories, and which one your job falls into

The Ministry of Justice groups the 94 occupations into four tiers, each with its own 2026 salary floor:

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>E-7-1, professional ("전문인력") — 67 occupations: managers and specialists such as software developers, engineers, architects, nurses, translators and university lecturers. 2026 floor: 31,120,000 won a year</li>
  <li>E-7-2, semi-professional ("준전문인력") — 10 occupations: office and service roles such as hotel front-desk clerks, tour guides, casino dealers, and head cooks. 2026 floor: 25,890,000 won a year</li>
  <li>E-7-3, general skilled ("일반기능인력") — 14 occupations: tradespeople such as shipyard welders, aircraft maintenance technicians and aquaculture technicians. 2026 floor: 25,890,000 won a year</li>
  <li>E-7-4, skilled worker ("숙련기능인력") — 3 occupations, reserved for E-9, E-10 and H-2 visa holders converting after years of work in Korea, covered in its own section below. 2026 floor: 26,000,000 won a year</li>
</ul>

Some occupations inside those tiers use a different wage rule entirely: shipyard welders and aquaculture technicians, for example, are pegged to 80% (or, for smaller employers, temporarily 70%) of the national per-capita GNI from two years prior rather than a flat won figure. Whichever number applies, your contract's hourly and monthly pay both still have to clear Korea's 2026 minimum wage — 10,320 won an hour, 2,156,880 won a month — and the contract itself has to state your daily and monthly working hours and total pay. This overview, the per-tier floors, and the minimum-wage cross-check all come from the Ministry of Justice's [E-7/E-7-4 visa guide portal](https://www.immigration.go.kr/sites/immigration/file/e7guide.html), the government's own self-diagnosis tool for this visa, current as of its November 2026 baseline update.

## What you personally have to show

For most of the 67 E-7-1 occupations, the baseline rule is one of three paths: a master's degree or higher in a related field, a bachelor's degree plus at least one year of related work experience earned after graduation, or five years of related experience with no degree requirement. Experience generally only counts from the date you earned the relevant degree or license — the exception is IT, bio and nanotech fields, where internship time before graduation can count, and bachelor's-plus-one-year occupations that also recognize broader field-placement experience.

Graduating from a Korean university changes the math. A bachelor's degree or higher from a Korean university (associate's degrees count too, but cyber/distance universities and technical colleges don't) waives the one-year experience requirement regardless of your major. A Korean associate's degree waives it only if your major matches the job. On top of the standard paths, the guide portal lists more than a dozen named exemptions, including: a Fortune 500 company role of a year or more, a bachelor's from a university ranked in the Times World 200 or QS 500, a salary at least 1.5 times the prior-prior-year per-capita GNI plus a government ministry's recommendation (education and experience both waived, for the 67 professional occupations only), and a salary at least 3 times that GNI figure, which waives education and experience regardless of occupation or recommendation. Every document proving a foreign degree, license or work history has to come with a translation and either an apostille from the issuing country or consular confirmation from a Korean embassy abroad — plan for that step before you assume your paperwork is ready.

## What your employer has to clear

Your own qualifications are half the filing. The other half is whether the hiring company can legally bring in a foreigner for that role at all. Most E-7 occupations are capped at 20% of the employer's "국민고용인원" — the count of Korean employees who have been on the employment-insurance roster for 3+ months and earn at least minimum wage. A company with fewer than 5 such employees and a domestic-market-only business is generally blocked outright, and if the math isn't exact (say, 12 Korean employees × 20% = 2.4), it rounds down below 2.5 and up from 2.5. The 67 E-7-1 professional occupations are mostly exempt from this cap and from company-size rules, applying only the salary floor — with named exceptions including mechanical engineers, drafters, web developers, travel-product developers, overseas sales staff, translators/interpreters and designers, which still face the quota.

A subset of occupations — travel-product developers, hotel front-desk clerks, medical coordinators, tour guides, aquaculture technicians, shipyard welders, and aircraft maintenance technicians among them — require a government ministry's employment recommendation letter attached at filing; it's optional for everything else, though a caseworker can still ask for one if the hiring need isn't obvious from the application. Universities, public institutions and large-company management hires are exempt from needing one at all. New companies get breathing room too: businesses otherwise subject to the Korean-employee quota generally need 3+ months on the employment-insurance roster first, but small foreign-invested and venture companies in manufacturing, trade, consulting and R&D get a 2-year grace period from founding, and the 67 professional occupations can be hired for up to 5 years with no sales record at all, provided supporting documents are filed.

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Confirm the exact occupation code among the 94 listed — "software developer" alone isn't enough; the portal distinguishes application developers, web developers, systems architects and more, each with its own rules</li>
  <li>Check whether your degree, university and salary trigger any of the named exemptions before assuming you need the full experience requirement</li>
  <li>Confirm your employer's Korean-employee headcount and 20% quota math, unless your occupation is one of the quota-exempt 67 professional roles</li>
  <li>Get any required recommendation letter before filing, not after — for the occupations where it's mandatory, an application without one is incomplete</li>
</ul>

## Applying: fees, steps and the 90-day registration deadline

The application sequence runs differently depending on where you're starting from. If you're abroad, your employer files for a Certificate of Confirmation of Visa Issuance (사증발급인정서) at their local immigration office or through the [visa portal](https://www.visa.go.kr), visa.go.kr; once issued, you take that certificate to a Korean embassy or consulate to get the actual visa stamped in your passport. If you're already in Korea on a convertible status — D-2 student or D-10 job-seeker among them — you file for a status change through [HiKorea's online portal](https://www.hikorea.go.kr) or in person instead. Either way, once you arrive or your change is approved, you have **90 days** to register for your Alien Registration Card at the immigration office covering your residence, bringing a photo, proof of address and a copy of the employer's business registration. Our [ARC guide](/how-to-get-arc-korea/) covers that registration step itself in full.

Status granted typically runs your contract length plus one month, capped at 3 years (some occupations are capped at 2 years or 1 year instead). None of the fees below are refundable once your application is accepted for review, since they cover the cost of the review itself, not the outcome:

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Certificate of Confirmation of Visa Issuance: free</li>
  <li>Visa issued at an embassy/consulate abroad: roughly USD 40 single-entry, USD 90 multiple-entry (varies by country)</li>
  <li>Status change (e.g., D-2 or D-10 to E-7): 100,000 won</li>
  <li>Extension of stay: 60,000 won (50,000 won if filed through HiKorea's e-application)</li>
  <li>Workplace change or addition — for occupations requiring prior approval: 120,000 won (free for occupations that only need post-change notification)</li>
  <li>Permission for activity outside your registered status: 120,000 won</li>
  <li>Alien Registration Card issuance or reissuance: 35,000 won</li>
  <li>Re-entry permit: 30,000 won single, 50,000 won multiple</li>
</ul>

How long the certificate or status-change review itself takes isn't fixed — the guide portal says it depends on document completeness, whether the company needs a site inspection, and whether your occupation is one that draws from an annual quota (hotel front-desk clerks, for instance, are capped at 400 nationwide; aquaculture technicians and slaughterhouse workers have their own caps too), which adds a head-office allocation step. Track your specific filing through the HiKorea portal rather than assuming a fixed number of weeks. All of the figures above — the fee schedule, the 90-day registration window, the quota math and the exemption list — come from the same Ministry of Justice guide portal cited earlier, which states its legal basis as Article 87 of the Immigration Control Act and Article 72 of its enforcement rule for the fee table specifically.

## E-7-4: converting from E-9, E-10 or H-2 after years on the job

If you've been working in Korea on a non-professional visa — E-9 (non-professional employment), E-10 (seafarer) or H-2 (working visit) — there's a separate point-based path into E-7-4 that doesn't require a new visa application at all, only a status change from inside Korea. To qualify you need all of the following at once: at least 4 years of combined, lawful time on E-9/E-10/H-2 status within the last 10 years (past and current stints add together, even with a gap abroad in between); a contract of 2+ years paying at least 26,000,000 won a year (25,890,000 won for agriculture, livestock, fishing and coastal-vessel work); a recommendation from an employer you've worked for for 1+ years; and a score of 200+ points on the ministry's point system, with income and Korean-language ability each required to clear a 50-point minimum on their own.

The Korean-language bar is TOPIK level 2, Social Integration Program (KIIP) stage 2 completion, or a pre-assessment placing you at stage 3 (score 41+) — through December 31, 2026, applicants who meet every other requirement can get a grace period to clear the language bar by their first E-7-4 extension, though family cannot join during that grace period and falling short caps the next extension at 6 months. Extra points are available too: 20 points for 3+ years working in one of the 89 officially designated population-declining regions or any rural township, and 20 points for a degree from a Korean distance university, cyber university, or credit-bank program. After converting, you're locked into the same occupation and employer's industry for 2 years (3 years if you converted under the non-capital-region exception) before a workplace change is allowed, and the application window itself only opens on a fixed weekday tied to the last digit of your birth year.

## When it goes wrong

**You're laid off or you quit.** Your employer has to report the change in your employment status. You can then convert to D-10 job-seeker status to look for a new sponsor, or your stay period gets adjusted toward departure if you don't. If the job ended for reasons the ministry treats as your own responsibility and the same thing happens again within a year, your next D-10 conversion can be refused — so a second self-inflicted exit within 12 months is a materially worse position than the first one.

**Your occupation requires prior approval for a workplace change and you didn't get it.** [Article 26-2 of the Immigration Control Act's enforcement decree](https://www.law.go.kr/법령/출입국관리법시행령) puts E-1 through E-7 holders under a workplace-change reporting duty set by Ministry of Justice notice; in practice, most E-7 occupations only need a notification within **15 days** of starting the new job. But roughly two dozen occupations — mechanical engineers, drafters, designers, sales clerks, head cooks, customer-service staff, hotel front-desk clerks, medical coordinators, aquaculture technicians, shipyard welders and several other manufacturing trades among them — need approval *before* you move, not after, and the new employer's quota and wage requirements get checked again from scratch. Confirm which rule applies to your specific occupation before you hand in notice.

**Your actual pay doesn't match your contract.** Extensions get checked against the income tax office's certificate of earned income, not your original contract. If what you were actually paid falls short of what the contract promised, your extension can be refused outright or limited to just 6 months — the same shortened outcome applies to employers with unpaid taxes or quota violations who've had to file a formal pledge to fix it.

**You're unsure which status you're even eligible to convert from.** Short-term visa categories (B/C series), technical training (D-3), seasonal work (E-8), E-9, E-10 and the catch-all G-1 status generally cannot convert directly to E-7 — E-9/E-10 holders can only reach E-7 through the E-7-4 point system described above, not the standard E-7-1/2/3 route. One exception: short-term-status holders applying for one of the 67 E-7-1 professional occupations specifically can convert directly, unless they hold the nationality of one of the countries the ministry has flagged for frequent overstays. If your current status isn't listed here, call the Immigration Contact Center at **1345** before filing rather than guessing.
