Found a used car you want and wondering whether your Alien Registration Card is actually enough to put it in your name? It is — a foreign registration number works the same way a Korean resident registration number does on every form involved. What trips people up isn't eligibility, it's the clock: the day you hand over money, a 15-day countdown starts, and two other things (insurance, tax) have to happen in a specific order around it, not just before the deadline.

## Yes, you can own and register a car on your ARC

Both halves of car ownership in Korea run on your Alien Registration Card instead of a 주민등록등본 (resident registration copy). The national vehicle-registration portal, [자동차365 (car365.go.kr)](https://www.car365.go.kr/ccpt/cvlcpt/guid/detailGuidance.do?cvlcptCd=4&_menuId=M610101005), lists "외국인" (foreigner) as a supported owner type for both new registration and ownership-transfer filings, and a district office's own transfer-registration guide confirms the document a foreign buyer brings in place of a resident registration copy is simply the ARC itself — see [Gwanak-gu's transfer registration page](https://www.gwanak.go.kr/site/gwanak/08/10802010200002016051206.jsp). Nothing about nationality changes the process from here; everything below applies the same way whether the buyer is Korean or not.

## Before you sign: check the car's registration ledger

Every registered car has a 자동차등록원부 (vehicle registration ledger) with two parts — 갑부 lists the ownership history and any 압류 (seizure), 을부 lists any 저당권 (lien) a bank or finance company holds against the car. You can look this up for free online through [gov.kr or 자동차365](https://www.car365.go.kr/ccpt/cvlcpt/guid/detailGuidance.do?cvlcptCd=15&_menuId=M610101014) before you commit to a used car — online lookup costs 0 won, while an in-person printout is 300 won and an in-person viewing is 100 won. Skipping this step is how people end up owning a car with someone else's loan attached to it.

Also worth knowing before you sign: if an insurer ever declared the car a total loss (전손), easylaw.go.kr's own guide to transfer registration states the registration office will only accept the transfer once that car has passed a 수리검사 (repair inspection) — a car with hidden total-loss history can get stuck in registration limbo until that's done.

## Insurance comes first, not after

This is the part people get backward. Korea's compulsory liability insurance (책임보험) has to exist before the registration office will touch your paperwork, and it has to be in the buyer's own name as the insured party — being added as a driver on someone else's policy doesn't count. Both [자동차365's guidance](https://www.car365.go.kr/ccpt/cvlcpt/guid/detailGuidance.do?cvlcptCd=4&_menuId=M610101005) and Gwanak-gu's transfer page state it plainly: the registration application requires a 의무보험가입증명서 (compulsory insurance certificate) attached, and "반드시 양수인이 피보험자로 가입하여야 합니다" — the buyer, not a co-signer, has to be the insured. Buy the policy first, then go file the transfer.

## Filing the transfer: documents, deadline, fees

For a used car bought from another private owner, [자동차관리법 제12조](https://easylaw.go.kr/CSP/CnpClsMain.laf?popMenu=ov&csmSeq=675&ccfNo=1&cciNo=2&cnpClsNo=3) sets the filing deadline at **15 days** from the purchase date (20 days for a car received as a gift, 6 months from the end of the month of death for an inheritance). The buyer brings:

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Transfer registration application (이전등록신청서)</li>
  <li>Transfer certificate from the seller (양도증명서)</li>
  <li>Seller's personal-seal certificate or signature-verification certificate, unless the sale went through a licensed dealer, an auction, or the registration office confirms the transfer directly</li>
  <li>Compulsory insurance certificate, already active, with the buyer as the insured</li>
  <li>The car's existing registration certificate (자동차등록증)</li>
  <li>ARC in place of a resident registration copy, for a foreign buyer</li>
</ul>

Gwanak-gu's office lists the processing fees as a 1,000 won local revenue stamp (1,500 won if you file in a different region from where the car is based), a 3,000 won national revenue stamp, and a new license plate running 7,500 to 21,700 won depending on type. On top of that comes acquisition tax (취득세): a standard non-commercial passenger car is taxed at **7%** of the purchase price, a compact 경차 at **4%**, and non-commercial cargo or van-type vehicles at **5%**, per [easylaw.go.kr's tax guide](https://easylaw.go.kr/CSP/CnpClsMain.laf?popMenu=ov&csmSeq=675&ccfNo=1&cciNo=1&cnpClsNo=2) and confirmed on Gwanak-gu's own fee table. General acquisitions get a 60-day window to file and pay this tax under [지방세법 제20조](https://www.gov.kr/portal/service/serviceInfo/PTR000051732), but a car is the exception the gov.kr guide calls out by name: you have to pay the acquisition tax before the registration office will register the car at all, which in practice means it's due inside the same 15-day window, not the general 60.

## Buying new instead: the temporary permit clock is shorter

A dealer selling you a brand-new car hands you a 임시운행허가 (temporary driving permit) so you can legally drive it off the lot before plates exist. For the purpose of getting to new registration, that permit runs **10 days** — confirmed on [Gimpo city's official vehicle registration page](https://www.gimpo.go.kr/portal/contents.do?key=1056), which lists the same figure as Gwanak-gu's processing-fee table. You're expected to submit the new-registration application within that 10-day window and hand the temporary permit back with it. Miss that window and the fine schedule mirrors the late-transfer one at a smaller scale: 30,000 won flat if you're within 10 days late, plus 10,000 won for every additional day beyond that, capped at 1,000,000 won.

## When it goes wrong

**You miss the 15-day transfer deadline.** The registration still has to happen — there's no way to just skip it — but a fine applies on a sliding scale. Up to 10 days late, it's a flat 100,000 won. Past 10 days, it's 100,000 won plus 10,000 won for every additional day, capped at 500,000 won. This schedule comes straight from Gwanak-gu's own 자동차관리법 과태료 처분기준 table, the same district office page cited above for documents and fees.

**The seller won't hand over documents, or has disappeared.** If the buyer doesn't file the transfer, Article 12(4) lets the seller file it instead, using their own personal-seal certificate marked for vehicle sale — a protection for sellers against a buyer who never finishes the paperwork, but it also means the process doesn't simply stall forever on one side's silence.

**The transfer application gets rejected.** Easylaw.go.kr's own guide lists two concrete grounds: the application contains false information, or (for a car an insurer wrote off as a total loss) it hasn't passed the required repair inspection yet. Both are fixable — correct the paperwork, or get the inspection done — rather than dead ends.

**You bought the car without checking the registration ledger first and found a lien after the fact.** This is the one failure mode that's hard to undo after signing, which is exactly why the free online ledger lookup above is worth doing before you pay, not after.

## Quick checklist before you sign anything

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Look up the registration ledger (갑부/을부) online for free — check for 압류 (seizure) and 저당권 (lien)</li>
  <li>If the car was ever a total loss, confirm it has passed a 수리검사 (repair inspection)</li>
  <li>Buy compulsory insurance in your own name before you file anything</li>
  <li>File the transfer within 15 days of the purchase date, with your ARC, the transfer certificate, and the insurance certificate</li>
  <li>Pay the acquisition tax (7% passenger, 4% compact, 5% cargo/van) before or at registration, not on the general 60-day schedule</li>
  <li>Budget 1,000–1,500 won in revenue stamps, 3,000 won in national stamp duty, and 7,500–21,700 won for plates</li>
</ul>
