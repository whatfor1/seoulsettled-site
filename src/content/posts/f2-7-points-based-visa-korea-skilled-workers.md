Three of our other guides mention F-2 in passing — as the status that counts toward F-5 permanent residency, as one of the categories exempt from [side-income filing](/freelance-income-tax-korea-foreigners/), as one of the statuses with mandatory [employment insurance](/unemployment-benefits-korea-foreign-workers/) — without ever explaining how an E-series or D-series visa holder actually gets there. For a specific group of people already working in Korea — registered under the same rule that gives every new arrival 90 days to get an Alien Registration Card — there's a direct route: F-2-7, a points-based system that lets you trade your current work visa for F-2 residence status, no new job offer or sponsor required. Here's who qualifies, how the score works, and what the two unresolved numbers in this scheme mean for your own application.

## What F-2-7 actually is

F-2-7 is one sub-category of the broader F-2 (거주, "residence") status, created under [출입국관리법 시행령 별표 1의2](https://law.go.kr/LSW/admRulLsInfoP.do?admRulSeq=2100000266296) item 24, 자목. The specific scoring criteria run on their own standalone notice, **법무부고시 제2025-408호**, which the Ministry of Justice published and put into effect on **October 24, 2025**. Unlike most work visas, F-2-7 isn't tied to a sponsor or an occupation list — once you're approved, your work rights are unrestricted, the same exemption from side-income filing that F-2, F-5 and F-6 holders already get under Article 20 of the Immigration Control Act.

## Who qualifies before the score even matters

Before your points get calculated, your current status has to be on the eligible list, and you have to have held it for long enough. Based on the notice's own eligibility criteria, cross-checked across multiple licensed labor-law and immigration-consultancy summaries of the scheme:

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>E-1 (professor), E-2 (conversation instructor), E-3 (research), E-4 (technical guidance), E-5 (professional), or E-6 (arts and entertainment, excluding E-6-2 venue performers) — held for 1 year or more</li>
  <li>D-5 through D-9 (journalism through corporate investment) — held for 1 year or more</li>
</ul>

If your current status isn't on that list — most commonly E-7 holders, who already have their own direct route to F-5 permanent residency instead — F-2-7 isn't the path for you, and applying anyway just wastes a review cycle.

## The score: five categories, one deduction, 125 points total

According to the points breakdown published by Korean labor-law and visa-consultancy firms who've summarized the notice (the law.go.kr page for 법무부고시 제2025-408호 only serves navigation and metadata when fetched directly — the scoring table itself sits in an attachment that doesn't render as text), the categories and their maximum points are:

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Age — up to 25 points</li>
  <li>Education — up to 35 points</li>
  <li>Korean language ability — up to 20 points</li>
  <li>Current income — up to 10 points</li>
  <li>Bonus points (career, social contribution, and similar factors) — up to 35 points</li>
  <li>Deduction — up to −5 points</li>
</ul>

That adds up to a possible 125 points, and the pass line sits at **80 points or more**. Three independent sources — a licensed labor attorney's published breakdown, and two separate visa-consultancy summaries — agree on this 125-point structure and the 80-point threshold. One earlier search result this guide checked and discarded claimed a 170-point scale instead; that number doesn't match any of the corroborated sources and most likely confuses F-2-7 with Korea's separate points-based F-5 permanent-residency track, which runs its own, unrelated scoring system. Where this guide can't independently confirm the exact point value for your specific age bracket, income tier, or bonus-point category — because no primary government page renders those brackets as readable text — the honest answer is to run your own numbers through HiKorea's status-by-status manual at [hikorea.go.kr](https://www.hikorea.go.kr) or call **1345** before you file, rather than estimate from a secondhand table.

## What it costs and how long it takes

The fee schedule here isn't F-2-7-specific — it's the same status-change fee structure [gov.kr's own civil-service listing](https://www.gov.kr/portal/service/serviceInfo/PTR000050536) sets for every registered foreigner changing status, which our [status-change guide](/change-visa-status-korea-foreigners/) also covers:

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Status-change application fee: 100,000 won (government revenue stamp)</li>
  <li>Alien Registration Card issuance for your new F-2 status: 35,000 won, per [gov.kr's ARC fee listing](https://www.gov.kr/portal/service/serviceInfo/PTR000050541) — the same fee our [ARC reissue guide](/arc-card-reissue-renewal-korea/) already confirmed</li>
  <li>Processing time: officially undetermined ("미정") per gov.kr, but typically takes at least 3 weeks and can run as long as 3 months</li>
</ul>

## Documents to gather before you book your HiKorea appointment

Based on the same consultancy breakdowns of the notice, expect to bring:

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Application form (서식 제34호, the standard status-change form)</li>
  <li>Passport and Alien Registration Card</li>
  <li>Degree certificate or diploma (high school diploma only if that's your highest level)</li>
  <li>Proof of Korean-language ability, such as a TOPIK score or a Social Integration Program (KIIP) completion record — KIIP's stage 0 runs 15 hours, its next four stages each run 100 hours, and its final stage splits into a basic track that runs 70 hours plus an enrichment track that runs 30 hours, the same program covered in our F-5 permanent-residency guide below</li>
  <li>Income proof and your employment contract</li>
  <li>Tax payment records</li>
  <li>Any career, volunteer-service, or other certificates that support a bonus-point claim</li>
</ul>

Book your visit through [HiKorea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr); applications go to the immigration office with jurisdiction over your registered address, the same office that handles [address change reports](/report-address-change-korea-arc-15-days/) if you've moved recently.

## The payoff: a faster route to F-5

Once you're approved for F-2-7 and spend 3 years on that status, you become eligible to apply for [F-5 permanent residency](/f5-permanent-residency-korea-foreigners/) — a shorter wait than the general route our F-5 guide covers, which requires 5 years on D-7 through E-7 or F-2 status before applying directly. That gap exists because F-2-7 is itself a selective, scored filter: the government is front-loading the vetting that the 5-year general route handles simply by waiting, which is also why F-2-7 holders already get F-2's unrestricted work rights immediately rather than only on reaching F-5.

## When it goes wrong

**Your score comes up short of 80.** The notice doesn't offer a partial-credit appeal — if your calculated score is under the pass line, the fix is to improve a scorable factor (finish a higher KIIP stage for the language points, document more qualifying income, gather a bonus-point certificate you hadn't submitted) and reapply once that factor changes, not to resubmit the same documents hoping for a different read.

**Your current status isn't actually on the eligible list.** If you're not sure whether your specific E or D sub-category counts — the boundaries around E-6-2 and the D-5 through D-9 range are exactly the kind of detail that changes between notice revisions — confirm with HiKorea's manual or **1345** before you assemble documents, since the 1-year holding requirement means a wrong guess here costs you a full review cycle, not just a form.

**Your application is denied for a different reason.** As with any status-change filing, the office issues a written reason for denial. Treat a denial tied to a specific document or eligibility item as fixable — gather the correct version and refile — rather than assuming the whole category is closed to you.

**You can't find a number this guide didn't print.** The exact point value for your age bracket, income tier, or a specific bonus-point category isn't something this guide asserts, because no primary government source renders it as readable text as of this writing. That's a genuine gap in public information, not an oversight — confirm your own numbers at HiKorea or 1345 rather than trust a blog's bracket table, including this one's.
