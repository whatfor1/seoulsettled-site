Search "Korea overseas remittance limit" right now and most of what comes up says $100,000 a year. That number is real, it started in January 2026, and it does not apply to you if you're here on a foreign passport. The Korea Federation of Banks' own guidance names the new $100,000 allowance for "residents, excluding foreign residents." Foreign residents are still working with the older $50,000 figure. This guide is the version for that second group: what the real limit is, what triggers paperwork, what it costs, and what to do if a transfer gets stuck.

## Resident or not: the six-month line

Korea's Foreign Exchange Transactions Act splits everyone into 거주자 (resident) and 비거주자 (non-resident), and the split is not about your visa type. A foreign national counts as a resident for foreign-exchange purposes once they've stayed in Korea 6 months or more, or if they're economically active here (working, running a business). Stay under 6 months and you're a non-resident, which comes with its own, more restrictive set of transfer rules — mainly built around proving the money came from Korea in the first place. Most readers of this guide, if you've already gone through [getting your ARC](/how-to-get-arc-korea/), will be on the resident side of that line.

## The actual number: $50,000 a year, no paperwork

For foreign residents, the no-documentation annual ceiling is $50,000. This is confirmed on two separate official pages: the Seoul Metropolitan Government's [banking guide for foreign residents](https://english.seoul.go.kr/service/living/convenience/1-banking), which states you may send up to $50,000 "including the exchange fee" out of the country, and the Korea Federation of Banks' own [bank-designation page](https://exchange.kfb.or.kr/page/exchange01.php), which cites the underlying rule directly: Article 4-4, paragraphs 1(3) and 2 of the Foreign Exchange Transaction Regulations, covering "payment of a foreigner or non-resident's domestic salary, income or pension, and payments of US$50,000 or less annually."

The January 2026 reform that everyone is writing about combined the old $100,000 bank-channel limit and $50,000 non-bank-channel limit into one $100,000 limit, tracked in real time through the Bank of Korea's new remittance-monitoring system (ORIS). But the Korea Federation of Banks titles the page describing that new $100,000 rule "거주자(외국인 거주자 제외)" — residents, foreign residents excluded. Nothing in that reform touched the separate $50,000 rule foreign residents use. If a bank teller quotes you $100,000, ask them to check again against your resident registration type.

## Under $5,000, nobody asks for documents

Below the annual cap, there's a second, smaller line that matters more day to day. Korean banks apply a per-transaction rule: any single transfer over roughly $5,000 requires documents showing where the money came from and why it's leaving the country. A transfer of $5,000 or less, sent to your own account or a relative's, generally goes through without that step. If you're sending rent deposit money home in chunks, this is the number to plan around — not the $50,000 annual figure.

## Register with one bank first: 거래외국환은행 지정

To use the $50,000 annual allowance without re-submitting proof every time, you register — once — with a single bank as your 거래외국환은행 (designated transaction bank). The Korea Federation of Banks lists this registration as mandatory for exactly this category of payment. You do it at a branch counter with your passport and residence card; after that, transfers within your annual allowance at that bank don't each need a fresh purpose document. If you later want to switch banks, the old branch has to confirm your registration before the new one will take you on — the KFB page describes this as a two-step handover between the old and new designated bank, so don't close the first account before the switch is confirmed.

> **Jun's tip:** Pick the bank where you already hold your salary account. You'll be back at that counter anyway for other paperwork, and the designation only helps if you actually use that bank for transfers.

## Sending more than $50,000

Above the annual no-document line, Seoul's banking guide says you can still send out an amount up to your documented Korean income — but you need to show it: an employment contract, pay slips, or a certificate of employment covering the period. Without that documentation, the bank stops at $50,000 for the year, full stop.

## What it actually costs

Fees are set per bank, not by regulation, so compare before you commit. Woori Bank's own published [foreign-exchange fee schedule](https://spot.wooribank.com/pot/Dream?withyou=FXFXG0025) is a useful benchmark for a branch outbound transfer: 5,000 won up to $500, 10,000 won up to $2,000, 15,000 won up to $5,000, 20,000 won up to $20,000, and 25,000 won above that. Sending through the bank's own app instead of a branch counter cuts that roughly in half — 2,500 won up to $500, 5,000 won up to $3,000, 7,500 won up to $5,000, and 10,000 won flat above $5,000 — and the 8,000 won cable fee (전신료) is waived entirely for app transfers. An incoming transfer from abroad costs 10,000 won at a branch or 5,000 won online. Most outbound wires land at the receiving bank within 1 to 3 business days, not instantly.

Licensed fintech remittance apps are the other common route, and they can beat bank rates on small transfers, but their fee tables change often enough that I'm not going to quote exact numbers here — check the fee and exchange rate the app shows you before you confirm, every time, since the number on the screen is the one that counts.

## If the transfer is rejected or your documents don't match

A rejected transfer is usually one of three things. First, a name mismatch: your passport, your ARC, and your bank registration all have to spell your name the same way, spacing included — this is the single most common reason a designated-bank registration gets refused. Second, a purpose document that doesn't match the amount: if your pay slips show less income than you're trying to send, expect the bank to cap the transfer at what the documents support, not what you asked for. Third, hitting the $50,000 ceiling without realizing it, usually because an earlier transfer this year already used part of it — ask the branch for your remaining balance before you assume you have the full amount left.

If your application is rejected for a fixable reason — wrong document, mismatched name, missing proof of income — the fix is almost always to bring the corrected paperwork back to the same branch rather than start over somewhere else, since your designation is already on file there. If something about the transfer looks wrong and you're not sure why, ask the branch to point to the specific regulation article they're applying; Korean banks are required to cite one, and "Article 4-4" or a similar number gives you something concrete to look up or appeal.

## Checklist before you send

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Confirm you've been in Korea 6 months or more (resident status for FX purposes)</li>
  <li>Check whether this transfer is under or over $5,000 — that decides if you need documents at all</li>
  <li>Register once with a single bank as your 거래외국환은행 if you plan to send more than one transfer</li>
  <li>Bring an employment contract or recent pay slips if you're sending more than $50,000 this year</li>
  <li>Compare the branch fee against the app fee — the discount is real but not automatic</li>
  <li>Ask your remaining annual balance before assuming you have the full $50,000 left</li>
</ul>
