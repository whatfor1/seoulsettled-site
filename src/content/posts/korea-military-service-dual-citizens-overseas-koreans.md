Having an F-4 visa does not, by itself, tell you whether Korea considers you conscription-eligible. F-4 (재외동포, "overseas Korean") status is legally only available to people who are *not* Korean nationals — but a lot of Korean-heritage families abroad have a son who was born a dual national (one Korean parent, foreign birthplace or foreign nationality acquired young) and never formally gave that up. That son is still a Korean national under the Nationality Act, cannot hold F-4 at all, and is subject to the Military Service Act (병역법) the same as any man born and raised in Seoul — the test is nationality, not which visa you think you'd qualify for.

This guide covers who that actually applies to, the two deadlines that decide whether you keep a choice in the matter, the overseas-residence deferral that most families rely on instead, and what happens if any of those windows gets missed.

## Who this is actually about

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Men who hold Korean nationality and a second nationality from birth (선천적 복수국적자) — typically because one parent was a Korean national when the child was born, regardless of where the birth happened</li>
  <li>Not F-4 holders whose heritage is real but whose Korean nationality was already lost or never held — the Act on Immigration and Legal Status of Overseas Koreans only issues F-4 to 외국국적동포 (foreign-nationality Koreans), and the Nationality Act does not let a current Korean national hold a foreign-national visa in the first place</li>
</ul>

If you're not sure which category you're in, the honest answer is to check your family's 가족관계증명서 (family relation certificate) or ask the nearest Korean consulate directly — don't assume your foreign passport settled the question, because under Korean law it didn't.

## The clock starts at 18, automatically

Every Korean national male is enrolled in 병역준비역 (the pre-service registry) on **January 1 of the year he turns 18** — this is Article 8 of the Military Service Act, confirmed directly on the Military Manpower Administration's own site, and it happens without any filing on your part. From that date, [Article 12 of the Nationality Act](https://casenote.kr/%EB%B2%95%EB%A0%B9/%EA%B5%AD%EC%A0%81%EB%B2%95/%EC%A0%9C12%EC%A1%B0) gives a dual national male only **3 months** to choose one nationality — in practice, that means by **March 31** of the year he turns 18, not the general complex-nationality deadline of **22 years old** that applies to dual nationals who aren't subject to military service at all.

Miss that 3-month window and Article 12③ is specific: you can only file a nationality-renunciation report later if you've completed active, reserve or alternative service, been placed in wartime labor service, or received a formal service exemption. In plain terms, the choice closes and doesn't reopen until the military obligation itself is resolved one way or another.

## The 2022 fix for people who missed it through no fault of their own

This used to be absolute. The Constitutional Court ruled on **September 24, 2020** that denying any renunciation path — regardless of why someone missed the 3-month window — was an excessive restriction on the freedom to leave one's nationality, and gave the legislature until September 30, 2022 to fix it. The result, in force since **October 1, 2022**, is Article 14-2 of the Nationality Act: an exceptional renunciation permit a dual national can apply for if he was born abroad (or moved abroad before age 6), has kept his main base of life abroad since then, and had a legitimate reason for missing the original 3-month deadline — commonly, that his parents never told him he was still a Korean national. The Minister of Justice decides case by case, weighing birthplace, how the dual nationality was acquired, and fairness to everyone else who did serve.

## If you stay abroad instead: the deferral path

Most families never deal with the renunciation deadline at all — they apply for an overseas-travel permit on 국외이주 (overseas emigration) grounds instead, which defers rather than resolves the obligation. Two separate tracks exist, and the numbers are different for each, so don't mix them up:

<ul class="checklist">
  <li><strong>General overseas-residence deferral:</strong> defers conscription until <strong>37 years old</strong> (extended from <strong>35 years old</strong> by a 2010 amendment to the Military Service Act); at <strong>38 years old</strong> you're moved to 전시근로역 (wartime labor service), which exempts you from active-duty callup in peacetime</li>
  <li><strong>재외국민 2세 (second-generation overseas national):</strong> a separate, narrower category for someone born abroad who stayed abroad with his parents continuously until age <strong>18</strong> and holds permanent residency or citizenship there — also exempted from <strong>38 years old</strong>, but the condition that revokes it is different: accumulating more than <strong>3 years</strong> of domestic residence in Korea after turning 18</li>
</ul>

The general deferral's own revocation trigger is different again: it breaks if you spend **6 months** or more in Korea within any 1-year period, or stay **60 days** or more while engaged in paid work — at which point the obligation is imposed and you're expected to complete your service like anyone else in the registry.

## When it goes wrong

**You're past 18 and never filed anything.** You're automatically in 병역준비역 whether or not you or your parents did any paperwork — there's no grace period that resets on its own. Contact the nearest Korean consulate or the Military Manpower Administration directly and ask what category you actually fall into before assuming either the renunciation window or an overseas-travel permit is still open for you.

**You missed the 3-month renunciation window and you're not sure the 2022 exception applies.** Article 14-2 only covers specific fact patterns — born abroad or moved abroad before age 6, continuous life abroad since, and a real reason for the miss. If your situation doesn't fit cleanly, don't guess; this is exactly the kind of case where a consulate or an immigration/nationality lawyer should look at your specific documents before you file anything.

**You went over the 6-month domestic-stay limit without realizing the clock was cumulative.** The limit counts total days across a rolling year, not one continuous trip — a few shorter visits can add up the same as one long one. If you're close to the line, check your actual travel record against the 1-year window before booking another trip home.

**You avoided the whole process and got caught.** Under [Article 94 of the Military Service Act](http://www.law.go.kr/lsLawLinkInfo.do?lsJoLnkSeq=1000451926&chrClsCd=010202), leaving or staying abroad without the required travel permit for the purpose of avoiding or reducing your service carries **1 to 5 years in prison**. A permit violation without that intent — for example, not returning within an approved travel period — is treated less severely: up to **1 year in prison or a fine of up to 10,000,000 won**. Either way, this isn't something to sort out by waiting and hoping; contact the Military Manpower Administration before a routine re-entry turns into an investigation.

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Confirmed whether you're a current Korean national (dual national) or already outside Korean nationality (F-4-eligible)</li>
  <li>If a dual national male: checked whether the March 31 renunciation deadline, in the year you turned 18, has already passed</li>
  <li>If it's passed, checked whether the 2022 exceptional-permit route (Article 14-2) could apply to your specific birthplace and residence history</li>
  <li>If relying on the overseas deferral instead, confirmed which of the two categories you're in — general (37/38, 6-month/60-day limits) or 재외국민 2세 (38, 3-year cumulative limit) — and tracked your actual days in Korea against it</li>
  <li>Saved your family relation certificate and any consulate correspondence in case your status is ever questioned at re-entry</li>
</ul>
