Our [year-end settlement guide](/year-end-tax-settlement-korea-foreign-workers/) covers the automatic reconciliation your employer runs on your salary every January. This one covers what happens when money arrives a different way — a freelance contract, tutoring on the side, consulting invoices, a YouTube channel that starts paying out. That income doesn't go through year-end settlement at all. It needs its own filing, called 종합소득세 (comprehensive income tax), and for most visa holders there's a second filing that has to happen first: permission to earn that income legally in the first place.

## The permission comes before the income, not after

[Article 20 of the Immigration Control Act](https://www.law.go.kr/LSW/lsLawLinkInfo.do?lsJoLnkSeq=1000822779&chrClsCd=010202) requires anyone who wants to engage in activity falling outside what their current status of stay covers to get the Minister of Justice's permission first — a 체류자격 외 활동허가 (permission for activity outside status). Most E-series work visas, D-2 student status and D-4 language-track status are tied to one specific job or school; taking freelance clients or running a side business on top of that is activity your current status doesn't cover, permission or not. Skip the filing and [Article 94, item 12](https://www.law.go.kr/LSW/lsLinkProc.do?lsNm=%EC%B6%9C%EC%9E%85%EA%B5%AD%EA%B4%80%EB%A6%AC%EB%B2%95&joNo=009400&mode=11) sets the penalty at **up to 3 years in prison or a 30,000,000 won fine** — the same ceiling the law sets for overstaying or for [starting a new status before a status-change is approved](/change-visa-status-korea-foreigners/).

[The gov.kr civil-service listing for this permission](https://www.gov.kr/portal/service/serviceInfo/PTR000050540) puts the fee at **120,000 won**, non-refundable once your application is accepted for processing, and states processing time as undetermined but "통상 3주에서 3개월가량 소요" — typically about 3 weeks to 3 months. Apply through a HiKorea appointment at the office with jurisdiction over your address, the same office that handles your [ARC](/how-to-get-arc-korea/). Some statuses are excluded from this permission entirely and simply can't add outside work no matter the paperwork, so confirm your specific status qualifies before you plan around freelance income — a HiKorea reservation or a call to **1345** settles it faster than guessing from someone else's visa story.

Some visa categories don't need this step at all: F-2 (long-term residency points-based), F-5 (permanent residency) and F-6 (marriage) holders already have unrestricted work rights, so freelancing doesn't require the Article 20 filing. If you're not sure which category you're in, that's a question for your immigration office before it's a question for a tax office.

## How the money actually gets taxed first

Freelance and consulting payments in Korea almost always arrive with **3%** already withheld at the source — [the National Tax Service's own page on business-income withholding](https://www.nts.go.kr/nts/cm/cntnts/cntntsView.do?mi=6622&cntntsId=7902) states the payer withholds 3% of the payment amount, and local income tax equal to 10% of that withheld amount is collected alongside it, bringing the combined rate to the **3.3%** figure most freelancers in Korea recognize from their pay statements. Whoever pays you is required to make this withholding and remit it to the NTS by the 10th of the following month — you don't have to do anything at the point of payment, but the amount withheld is only a prepayment, not your final tax bill.

## The May filing settles what withholding didn't

Each spring, that 3.3% gets reconciled against your actual tax liability through 종합소득세 — comprehensive income tax. Unlike year-end settlement, which your employer runs for you, this filing is yours to make. For income earned in 2025, [a National Tax Service filing-season announcement](https://sc.nts.go.kr) confirms the 2026 deadline as **June 1** for general filers (the statutory May 31 cutoff falls on a weekend, pushing it to the next business day) and **June 30** for people classified as 성실신고확인대상자 — generally higher-revenue self-employed filers who are required to have a tax agent verify their return before submission.

Korea's income tax uses eight progressive brackets. [The National Tax Service's own 종합소득세 rate table](https://www.nts.go.kr/nts/cm/cntnts/cntntsView.do?cntntsId=7667&mi=2227) sets the 2025-income brackets at **6%** on taxable income up to 14,000,000 won, climbing through 15%, 24%, 35%, 38%, 40% and 42% to a top rate of **45%** above 1,000,000,000 won (one billion won), with a progressive deduction subtracted at each step so you're not taxed at the top rate on your entire income, only the portion inside that bracket. A freelancer with 30,000,000 won in taxable income after expenses, for instance, lands in the 15% bracket with a 1,260,000 won deduction: 30,000,000 × 15% − 1,260,000 = 3,240,000 won before the 3.3% already withheld is credited against it.

Taxable income isn't your gross freelance revenue — you subtract 필요경비 (necessary expenses) first, either tracked through actual bookkeeping or, for filers who didn't keep books, estimated under a simplified or standard expense-rate system that varies by the type of work. Which system applies and what rate it uses depends on your business code, so this is one of the places a tax agent or Hometax's own guided filing earns its fee — getting the expense category wrong changes which bracket you land in.

## What it costs to file late or not at all

[Article 47-2 of the Framework Act on National Taxes](https://casenote.kr/법령/국세기본법/제47조의2) sets the non-filing penalty at **20%** of the unpaid tax for an ordinary missed filing, rising to **40%** if the non-filing involves fraud or other improper means, and **60%** if the undisclosed income involves offshore assets or accounts. On top of that, [the National Tax Service's own penalty guidance](https://www.nts.go.kr/nts/cm/cntnts/cntntsView.do?mi=2276&cntntsId=7697) confirms a separate late-payment penalty accrues daily at a rate of **22/100,000 (0.022%)** of the unpaid amount for every day it stays unpaid — small per day, but it compounds for as long as the balance sits unpaid, and it stacks on top of the non-filing penalty rather than replacing it. Concretely: on 2,000,000 won of unpaid tax, the 20% non-filing penalty alone adds 400,000 won, and 90 days of unpaid balance at 0.022% a day adds roughly another 39,600 won on top — filing 90 days late costs meaningfully more than filing 9 days late, since both penalties scale with however long the filing sits undone.

</ul>

## When it goes wrong

**You started freelancing before getting Article 20 permission.** Stop taking new client work under the unapproved status immediately and apply for the permission now — the exposure doesn't disappear by applying late, but continuing to add unauthorized income after you know the rule only adds to it. If an immigration office raises it during an unrelated filing (an extension or ARC renewal, for instance), answer directly rather than guessing what they already know.

**You missed the June filing deadline.** The 20% non-filing penalty and the 0.022%-a-day late-payment charge both start running from the day after the deadline, not from whenever you notice. File as soon as you catch the miss — the penalty calculation is based on how many days elapse, so every day of delay after you've spotted the problem adds to the bill for no benefit.

**Your sources disagree on how much withholding you should credit.** Match the 3.3% figures your clients reported against your own payment records before you file; a client's withholding report to the NTS and what actually landed in your account can diverge if a client misreports your resident registration or business number. Hometax's "지급명세서" lookup under your own login shows what's actually been reported against your name — reconcile against that rather than your memory of each invoice.

**You're not sure if your situation needs a tax agent.** 성실신고확인대상자 status depends on revenue thresholds that vary by industry and change periodically. Rather than estimate your own threshold, Hometax's guided filing flags this status automatically once you enter your revenue, or a call to your local tax office settles it before you commit to a filing method.
