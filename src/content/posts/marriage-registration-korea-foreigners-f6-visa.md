Registering a marriage and applying for a spouse visa feel like one errand, but Korean law runs them through two completely different offices, two completely different document lists, and — this is the part that trips people up — two different clocks. Get the order wrong and you can end up with a legally registered marriage and no visa to show for it, or a visa application an office won't even accept because the marriage itself was never filed.

## Two different processes, two different offices

**혼인신고 (marriage registration)** is a civil filing with Korea's family-relations register, handled through a district or town office (구청, 시청, or 읍·면·동 주민센터) or, if the marriage happened abroad, through the nearest Korean embassy or consulate. It makes the marriage legally exist in Korea's records. It has nothing to do with immigration status.

**The F-6 visa** is a residence permit issued under the Immigration Control Act, applied for separately at a Korean embassy or consulate abroad (for a foreign spouse entering for the first time) or through a status change at an immigration office (for someone already in Korea on another visa). It requires the marriage to already be registered — the civil filing comes first.

## Registering the marriage itself

**If you're both in Korea.** File in person or by mail at any district or town office, using the standard 혼인신고서 (Form No. 10). Korea's own civil-service listing for the filing, [confirmed on gov.kr](https://www.gov.kr/mw/AA020InfoCappView.do?CappBizCD=12700000050), states the fee as **수수료 없음** — no fee — and the processing standard as "지체없이" (without delay, within the office's available capacity), with the Supreme Court's family-registry division as the agency behind the rule. A Korean national filing with a foreign spouse additionally needs the foreign spouse's 혼인요건구비증명서 (a certificate from their home country or its embassy confirming they're legally free to marry) and its Korean translation, plus proof of nationality such as a passport.

**If the marriage already happened abroad.** You still have to report it to Korea separately — a foreign marriage certificate doesn't automatically appear in the Korean family register. Under [Article 35 of the Act on Registration of Family Relations](https://ko.wikisource.org/wiki/%EB%8C%80%ED%95%9C%EB%AF%BC%EA%B5%AD_%EA%B0%80%EC%A1%B1%EA%B4%80%EA%B3%84%EC%9D%98_%EB%93%B1%EB%A1%9D_%EB%93%B1%EC%97%90_%EA%B4%80%ED%95%9C_%EB%B2%95%EB%A5%A0), a Korean national who marries abroad under that country's own legal process must submit a certified copy of the marriage certificate to the nearest Korean embassy or consulate within **3 months**. The Korean Embassy in London's own [marriage-registration procedure page](https://gbr.mofa.go.kr/gb-ko/brd/m_8376/view.do?seq=1305135) spells out what that filing looks like in practice: the original UK-issued marriage certificate, a signed Korean translation, both passports, and — by visit or by mail — a processing time the embassy quotes at **about 3 weeks** from the day the embassy receives the file.

## What happens if you skip the 3-month window

Article 122 of the same Act sets the penalty for missing a registration deadline without a valid reason at **a fine of up to 50,000 won** — small, but the deadline itself is what matters, because an unregistered marriage can't support an F-6 filing later. If an office formally demands the overdue filing under Articles 38 or 108 and you still don't act within the window it sets, a second fine of **up to 100,000 won** applies. Neither amount is large enough to plan around; the real cost of missing the window is the delay it adds before you can even start the visa side of the process.

## The F-6 visa: the income test most people don't know about

Once the marriage is on record, a foreign spouse still outside Korea applies for the F-6 visa at a Korean embassy or consulate in their home country. The part that catches people off guard is the Korean sponsor's income requirement. The [Korean Consulate General in Shanghai's own 2026 guidance](https://overseas.mofa.go.kr/ch-ko/brd/m_20652/view.do?seq=5) publishes the household-income table that Justice Ministry notice sets for sponsors:

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>2-person household (sponsor and spouse alone): 25,195,752 won a year</li>
  <li>3-person household: 32,154,216 won a year</li>
  <li>4-person household: 38,968,428 won a year</li>
  <li>5-person household: 45,340,314 won a year</li>
  <li>6-person household: 51,335,712 won a year</li>
  <li>7-person household: 57,090,900 won a year, plus 5,755,188 won for each additional member</li>
</ul>

Household size isn't just "how many people live together" — the same guidance counts only the sponsor, the incoming spouse, and direct-line relatives (grandparents, parents, children, grandchildren) who share the sponsor's 주민등록표 household registration. Siblings don't count. Counted income is the sponsor's total from the past year across wage income, business income, rental income, interest, dividends, and pension income combined, not salary alone — so a sponsor short on payroll income can sometimes still qualify by adding other income types.

Fees and processing times vary mission by mission, the same way the apostille process does. The [Korean Consulate General in Chicago's own F-6 guidance](https://down.mofa.go.kr/us-chicago-ko/brd/m_4760/view.do?seq=1060967&page=2) quotes **45 USD**, cash or money order only, for US applicants, with documents submitted in person and processing taking **about 1 month**. Check your own country's Korean mission page rather than assuming either figure travels across borders.

## The mandatory class some nationalities have to take first

For a specific list of nationalities, the visa office won't process the F-6 application at all until the Korean sponsor completes the **국제결혼 안내프로그램** (International Marriage Guidance Program). Korea's own government-news portal [confirms the rule](https://m.korea.kr/briefing/pressReleaseView.do?2018-06-02=&newsId=155758288&pageIndex=3526&repCode=&repCodeType=&srchWord=&startDate=2008-02-29): mandatory since **March 7, 2011**, for Korean nationals inviting a spouse from China, Vietnam, the Philippines, Cambodia, Mongolia, Uzbekistan, or Thailand. The program covers the spouse's home-country culture and etiquette, the F-6 visa procedure itself, and — a section added specifically — education on spousal rights and preventing domestic violence.

Exemptions exist: couples who can show they lived together for **45 days or more** outside Korea, or that the foreign spouse was already registered and living in Korea for **91 days or more** while the relationship was documented, don't have to attend. Humanitarian cases, including pregnancy, are also exempted. If none of those apply to your situation, book the class before you file rather than after — it's a precondition for the embassy to accept the application at all, not paperwork you can add later.

## If you're already in Korea on a different visa

Marrying while already in Korea on a D-2, E-7, or other status doesn't require leaving the country to apply for F-6 from abroad. Instead it runs through the status-change process covered in my [guide to changing visa status](/change-visa-status-korea-foreigners/): a **30-day** filing deadline from the date of the marriage under Article 24(2) of the Immigration Control Act, and a **100,000 won** fee (**200,000 won** if the change is to [F-5 permanent residency](/f5-permanent-residency-korea-foreigners/) instead). If the marriage certificate behind that filing was issued outside Korea, it may also need authentication before the immigration office will accept it — see my [guide to apostilles and document authentication](/apostille-documents-korea-foreigners/) for which path applies depending on where it was issued.

## When it goes wrong

**You let the 3-month report window pass.** The fine itself — up to 50,000 won, rising to 100,000 won if you ignore a formal notice — isn't what costs you. An unregistered marriage can't support an F-6 filing, so every day past the deadline is a day the visa clock hasn't even started. File the report the moment you have the certified foreign marriage certificate in hand, not after you've settled in.

**Your F-6 application is rejected.** Embassies don't always explain a denial in detail on the visa decision itself. If your application is rejected, ask the issuing mission directly what the stated reason was before you reapply, since a second attempt with the same gap in documentation or income will likely fail the same way.

**You skip the mandatory class because you didn't know it applied to you.** If your spouse's nationality is on the designated list and neither exemption applies, the consulate can simply refuse to process the F-6 paperwork until the sponsor's completion certificate is on file — confirm your specific country's status before you assume the class doesn't apply.

**The sponsor's income falls just short of the household threshold.** The guidance explicitly combines wage, business, rental, interest, dividend, and pension income rather than salary alone, so check every income category before concluding you don't qualify — and if you're still short, ask the consulate directly what alternatives, if any, apply to your case rather than guessing.
