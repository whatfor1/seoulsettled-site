Our [E-7 guide](/e7-visa-korea-specialized-worker-requirements/) and [status-change guide](/change-visa-status-korea-foreigners/) both mention E-2 (회화지도, "foreign language instructor") in passing without walking through what it actually requires. E-2 is the standard visa for teaching a foreign language — usually English, but also French, German, Japanese, Chinese and others — at a hagwon, public or private school, university language program, broadcasting company, or corporate language center. Unlike E-7, which covers dozens of occupations under one framework, E-2 is narrow and tightly tied to a single employer and contract: the paperwork is more predictable, but the screening is heavier than most other work visas, because it runs through an extra layer most E-series visas skip — a mandatory health check — on top of the usual degree and background-check requirements.

## Who qualifies: the degree rule is codified, the nationality list isn't

The legal basis for the degree requirement sits in the Enforcement Decree of the Act on the Establishment and Operation of Private Teaching Institutes and Extracurricular Lessons (학원의 설립·운영 및 과외교습에 관한 법률 시행령), Table 3 (학원강사의 자격기준) — foreign instructors teaching domestically must hold proper status under the Immigration Control Act (Article 10, 12, 20 or 25) and at least a bachelor's degree or an equivalent recognized under the Higher Education Act. In practice, your degree doesn't need to be in education or even in the language you'll teach — a bachelor's in any field generally clears this bar, as long as it's a real, verifiable degree with an apostille or consular authentication attached.

The nationality restriction most people have heard of — commonly cited as US, UK, Canada, Ireland, Australia, New Zealand and South Africa — is a different story. I checked for a current, fetchable government page codifying this exact seven-country list and couldn't find one; it's consistently repeated across recruiter and visa-consultancy sites, which suggests it reflects long-standing Ministry of Justice administrative screening practice rather than a line item in the enforcement decree itself. I'm not printing it here as a confirmed government figure. If your passport isn't one of those seven, don't assume you're excluded and don't assume you're included — confirm directly with your sponsoring institution or a HiKorea caseworker before you commit to a job offer built around it.

## The paperwork: CCVI, background check, then the consulate stamp

If you're applying from outside Korea, your employer files for a Certificate of Confirmation of Visa Issuance (사증발급인정서, CCVI) at their local immigration office or through HiKorea first. [Gov.kr's own civil-service listing for this filing](https://www.gov.kr/mw/AA020InfoCappView.do?HighCtgCD=A10001&CappBizCD=12700000104) confirms the fee as **면제** — exempt, no charge. The same listing doesn't commit to a clean processing-time figure the way some other filings do, so budget weeks rather than days and track your specific application through HiKorea instead of planning around a fixed clock.

Your criminal background check has its own deadline, and it changed in 2021. An official consular notice (게시일 2021-09-07, based on an underlying notice dated 2021-09-06) confirms: "사증발급·체류자격 변경허가·체류기간 연장허가 신청일로부터 6개월 이내 발급된 서류일 것(기존 3개월 → 6개월)" — the document has to be issued within **6 months** of your application date, extended from the old 3-month window, and the rule applies to visa issuance, status-change, and extension applications alike. Get your background check and its apostille done close to when you'll actually file, not months in advance "to be safe" — too early and it expires before the embassy sees it.

Once the CCVI is approved, you take it to a Korean consulate abroad for the actual visa stamp. [The Korean Consulate General in New York's E-2 page](https://overseas.mofa.go.kr/us-newyork-ko/brd/m_4217/view.do?seq=708743) states a **$45** fee, paid by money order or cash depending on the consulate, with a quoted processing time of **14 days** for that specific office — other consulates may differ, so check your own consulate's posted schedule rather than assuming New York's number applies everywhere. The same page states the maximum stay period E-2 can be granted as **up to 2 years, within your contract period plus 1 month** — not an automatic 2 years regardless of contract length.

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Confirm your bachelor's degree (any field) is apostilled or consular-authenticated before your employer files the CCVI</li>
  <li>Time your criminal background check so it's issued within 6 months of your actual application date, not your offer date</li>
  <li>Check your specific consulate's E-2 fee and processing window — $45 and 14 days are confirmed for New York, not every post</li>
  <li>Don't assume the commonly-cited 7-country nationality list applies to your passport without confirming with your employer or HiKorea first</li>
</ul>

## The health check: TB, a four-drug panel, and a test that was abolished in 2017

E-2 carries a mandatory health check most other work visas don't require in the same form. A tuberculosis (TB) test result is required for stays of 91 days or longer and is only valid for **90 days (3 months)** from a designated hospital — get it close to your filing date, the same timing problem as the background check. Immigration's drug-test panel for E-2 applicants screens for four substances under a 2017 Ministry of Justice notice (No. 2017-116): **MA** (methamphetamine), **COC** (cocaine), **OPI** (opium) and **THC** (cannabis).

That same 2017 notice is also the one that abolished the HIV test that had been mandatory for E-2 applicants since 2011 (under the earlier Notice No. 2011-23). The change took effect **July 3, 2017**, following a recommendation from Korea's National Human Rights Commission, which found the HIV-testing requirement discriminatory — it singled out E-2 holders among foreign workers without a corresponding public-health rationale that applied to Korean nationals in the same jobs. If you're researching E-2 and run into older blog posts or forum threads describing an HIV test, that requirement no longer exists.

## What E-2 doesn't let you do

E-2 is tied to one employer and the specific institution named on your CCVI — it isn't general work authorization. Teaching a private one-on-one lesson for cash, picking up a second hagwon's hours without your current employer's and immigration's sign-off, or running paid online classes outside what your contract covers all fall under the same rule as any other unauthorized activity: [Article 20 of the Immigration Control Act](https://www.law.go.kr/LSW/lsLawLinkInfo.do?lsJoLnkSeq=1000822779&chrClsCd=010202) requires Minister of Justice permission before taking on activity your status doesn't already cover, and [Article 94, item 12](https://www.law.go.kr/LSW/lsLinkProc.do?lsNm=%EC%B6%9C%EC%9E%85%EA%B5%AD%EA%B4%80%EB%A6%AC%EB%B2%95&joNo=009400&mode=11) sets the penalty at **up to 3 years in prison or a 30,000,000 won fine** for doing it without that permission — our [freelance income tax guide](/freelance-income-tax-korea-foreigners/) covers the same 120,000 won outside-activity permit this rule routes through for side income generally. In practice, immigration rarely grants outside-activity permission for private tutoring specifically, since the entire framework around E-2 exists to keep foreign-language instruction inside institutions immigration can inspect and hold accountable — so "ask first" for private lessons usually means "the answer is no," not "wait for approval."

## When it goes wrong

**Your background check or TB test expires before your visa is actually issued.** Processing delays at either the CCVI stage or the consulate stage can eat into a document's validity window faster than expected. If either is going to lapse before your appointment or departure date, get a fresh one rather than submitting an expired document and hoping no one checks — a rejected application for an expired document costs you more time than ordering the replacement early would have.

**Your nationality or degree doesn't match what a recruiter told you was standard.** Since the seven-country list isn't something I could confirm on a government page, treat any recruiter's confident claim about your eligibility as a starting point, not a guarantee. Call **1345** (외국인종합안내센터) or ask your prospective employer to confirm with their immigration caseworker before you give notice at your current job or book a flight.

**You got caught doing unpaid-for or under-the-table private tutoring.** Stop immediately — continuing after you know the rule only adds to the exposure under Article 94. If immigration raises it during an unrelated filing (an extension, for instance), answer directly. This is a materially worse position to be in than never having started, and talking your way around it rarely helps once it's already been flagged.

**Your contract ends early — you're laid off, the hagwon closes, or you resign.** Your E-2 status is tied to that specific employer, so losing the job affects your legal basis to stay. You'll generally need either a new employer to sponsor a fresh CCVI/status filing (100,000 won) or a transition to D-10 job-seeking status (60,000 won to extend, once on it); see our [status-change guide](/change-visa-status-korea-foreigners/) for how that filing works and [D-10 job-seeking visa guide](/d10-job-seeking-visa-korea-foreigners/) if you need time to find a new sponsor. Don't keep teaching for the old employer, paid or not, once the underlying contract has actually ended.
