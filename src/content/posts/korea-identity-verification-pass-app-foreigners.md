Every banking app, government site and delivery platform in Korea stops at some point and asks you to prove you're really you — a popup called 본인인증 (identity verification) that checks your name, phone and sometimes a bank account against each other, character for character. Koreans breeze through it because the whole system is built around their resident registration number. Foreigners don't get that number — we get an alien registration card instead, with its own deadline of **90 days** to register and **15 days** to report an address change — and PASS, Kakao인증 and the bank-issued financial certificate were all designed for a number most of us never carry. Here's how each one actually works, what a foreigner needs to set it up, and what to do when the check fails.

## Why one popup breaks three different apps

Until **December 10, 2020**, one kind of certificate ran almost everything: the 공인인증서 (certified authentication certificate), a government-designated monopoly that had been mandatory since 1999. Korea's Electronic Signature Act was amended that day, ending the certificate's **21 years** as the only option with legal standing, according to the government's own announcement on [korea.kr](https://www.korea.kr/news/policyNewsView.do?newsId=148880731). In its place came a competitive field: 공동인증서 (the renamed successor to the old certificate), 금융인증서 (issued by Korea's Financial Settlement Institute through your bank), and private certificates built by Kakao, Naver, Toss and the telecom-run PASS app — all now carrying equal legal weight, none required over another.

That history matters for a practical reason: more than **5 years** after the change, most Korean apps still let you pick which certificate to use, and each one has different requirements for foreigners. Picking the wrong one first wastes a trip to a bank branch or an afternoon stuck on a verification screen.

## PASS: the carrier-run default

PASS is the joint identity app run by Korea's three mobile carriers — SK Telecom, KT and LG U+ — and it's usually the first verification method any Korean app offers. Per the app's own [official FAQ](https://www.passauth.co.kr/question), foreigners can use PASS "if they can receive mobile phone identity verification and can register for the authentication app" — there's no blanket foreigner exclusion written into the service terms. The same FAQ sets the minimum age at **14 years old** and requires **Android 6.0** or **iOS 12.0** or newer.

The catch isn't PASS itself — it's what phone line you're registering it to. Our own [guide to getting a Korean phone number](/korean-phone-number-foreigners-prepaid-postpaid/) found that a passport-registered prepaid SIM often fails the identity checks banks and other apps run, because that line isn't tied to your real-name alien registration record the way a postpaid or MVNO line is. PASS inherits that same limitation: it verifies against whatever name and number your carrier has on file, so a prepaid bridge line can register the app but then fail the actual verification step downstream. Move to a postpaid or MVNO plan once your ARC arrives, and most PASS verification failures clear up on their own.

## Kakao인증: built on a phone and a bank account, not KakaoTalk

Kakao인증 looks like it should need the KakaoTalk app installed, but the official eligibility page is narrower and more specific than that. Kakao's own customer support page states the requirement directly: anyone who has "a mobile number properly registered with a domestic carrier in their own name, and a Korean bank account in their own name" can use the certificate — confirmed on [Kakao's official help page](https://cs.kakao.com/helps_html/1073198854?locale=ko). That's two conditions, not one, and both have to be in your name exactly as it appears on your other Korean records.

In practice this means Kakao인증 is usually the second step, not the first: get a real-name Korean phone number, open a [Korean bank account](/open-bank-account-korea-foreigner/), then set up Kakao인증 once both exist. Trying to register it with only a prepaid number or no bank account yet is the single most common reason the setup screen rejects a foreigner's first attempt.

## 금융인증서: the bank-issued certificate that outlasts the others

The financial certificate (금융인증서) is issued through your own bank's app once you already hold a Korean bank account and a phone registered in your own name to receive the verification text. KB국민은행's own certificate-center page confirms the certificate's validity runs **3 years** from issuance, and can be renewed starting **90 days** before it expires and up until the expiry date itself, per [KB국민은행's financial certificate guide](https://obank.kbstar.com/quics?page=C110573). That's longer than most people expect — the older certified-certificate system required a renewal every 12 months, so the financial certificate is deliberately built to need less maintenance.

Once issued, a financial certificate works across more than just banking apps — [gov.kr's own FAQ on the 2020 certificate changes](https://www.gov.kr/portal/faq/3200) confirms the government added 금융인증서 as a login option for Gov24 (정부24) alongside 공동인증서, on top of the banking, card and investment apps it was originally built for. If you're going to set up only one certificate beyond PASS, this is usually the one worth the extra bank-branch step, precisely because it doesn't expire every year like the certificate it replaced.

## Which one to set up first

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Get a Korean phone number in your own name — prepaid is fine to start, but verification-heavy apps will eventually need a postpaid or MVNO line tied to your ARC</li>
  <li>Install PASS first — it needs only the phone line above and works for most everyday age and identity checks</li>
  <li>Open a Korean bank account, then register Kakao인증 if you already use KakaoTalk daily</li>
  <li>Once the bank account is a few weeks old, set up the 금융인증서 through your bank's app — it's valid for 3 years and can be renewed starting 90 days before expiry, so it needs far less upkeep than a one-time code</li>
  <li>Keep your name spelled identically — carrier, bank and certificate — since a mismatch anywhere fails the whole chain</li>
</ul>

## When it goes wrong

**Your name doesn't match across accounts.** Verification checks your name against your phone carrier, your bank and your ID character for character, spacing included. Our [credit card guide](/credit-card-korea-foreigners/) covers this exact failure mode on banking and card apps — fix the spelling at whichever record is wrong, then retry rather than re-submitting the same mismatch.

**PASS or Kakao인증 won't finish registering.** This usually means the phone line behind it is still a passport-only prepaid SIM. Switch to a postpaid or MVNO plan once your ARC arrives — the number-portability fee to move carriers and keep your number is a flat **800 won** plus VAT, confirmed on the carrier side in our [phone number guide](/korean-phone-number-foreigners-prepaid-postpaid/), so there's no real cost reason to delay the switch once you're eligible.

**The bank app rejects your 금융인증서 application.** This is almost always a name- or document-mismatch issue rather than an eligibility one — bring your ARC and ask the branch to confirm exactly which field doesn't match before you try again from home. A rejected application can usually be resubmitted the same day once the branch tells you what was wrong.

**A form only has a field for "주민등록번호" and yours is an alien registration number.** Both numbers share the same 13-digit yymmdd-based format, so the field itself can usually hold either one — but whether the site's backend actually accepts a foreigner-registration number in that field varies site by site. If it's rejected outright, that's worth a call to the service's own support line rather than assuming the service excludes foreigners entirely; several of the sites behind these certificates do support foreign users, as the sourcing above shows.

**You never installed KakaoTalk and don't want to.** Kakao인증's own eligibility rule doesn't mention KakaoTalk at all — it's phone number and bank account only — so you may be able to register the certificate through Kakao's standalone authentication flow without ever opening the messaging app, though in practice most people already have KakaoTalk installed by the time they need this.

**You're on a passport-only prepaid line and don't want to switch carriers yet.** You don't have to rush it — a prepaid SIM can carry you for weeks while you sort out housing and a bank account. But once you do switch, moving carriers while keeping your number costs a flat **800 won** plus VAT, so there's no financial reason to put it off once you're eligible.

**Your ARC registration itself is overdue.** Every certificate above ultimately checks your record against a registered alien registration card, so if you haven't filed yet — you have **90 days** from entry to do it — that's the problem to fix before troubleshooting any single app.

**You moved and never reported the new address.** A stale address on your ARC record can also throw off checks tied to it. Report a move to your local 주민센터 within **15 days**, the same window used for most residence-record updates.

The one rule that doesn't change: everything downstream depends on a real-name Korean phone line, a bank account, and an ARC record that's actually up to date — the same **90 days** to register and **15 days** to report a move that govern the rest of your paperwork here. Fix the phone line first; the certificates follow from there.
