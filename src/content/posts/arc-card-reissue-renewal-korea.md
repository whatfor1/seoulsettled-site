Ten years is how long a permanent residence card stays valid before Korea's Immigration Act requires a new one — and that's the easy case, with a decade of warning. Lose the card, crack it, or have your passport details change, and the window is much shorter: 14 days, not ten years. Reissuing an ARC is a different process from the first-time registration most guides cover, with its own deadline, its own fee, and its own way of going wrong if you wait.

This guide covers who needs a reissued card, what triggers the clock, what it costs, and the specific 10-year rule that only applies to permanent residents (F-5). I'm Jun, a Seoul local, and every figure below is checked against the Ministry of Justice's own service listing and the Immigration Act itself.

## What counts as "reissuance," and what doesn't

Reissuance (재발급) is not the same filing as your original [ARC registration](/how-to-get-arc-korea/) or a [stay-period extension](/extend-stay-period-korea-visa-extension-hikorea/). It's the process for getting a new physical card when your current one is still technically valid as a status, but the card itself is the problem. Korea's official government service portal, [gov.kr](https://www.gov.kr/portal/service/serviceInfo/PTR000050541), lists five triggers for reissuance:

- Your card is **lost**.

- Your card is **damaged or too worn to read**.

- There's **no blank space left** on the card for new entries or stamps.

- You received a **change of visa status**.

- Your **name, gender, date of birth, or nationality changed**.

A sixth case sits outside that list but matters just as much: if you hold **F-5 permanent residence**, your card itself expires every 10 years even though your immigration status never does. More on that below.

## Step 1: Know your 14-day clock

Korean administrative-law guides consistently cite **14 days** from the date of the triggering event — loss, damage, or an information change — as the deadline to apply for reissuance at the immigration office covering your registered address. One English-language HiKorea walkthrough cites 15 days instead; the sources don't fully agree, so treat 14 days as your real deadline and don't bank on the extra day. Missing the window can carry an administrative fine, but neither the government service listing nor the guides I checked publish one consistent amount — call the Immigration Contact Center at **1345** if you've already gone past it, rather than guessing at the number.

If your card was lost rather than damaged, filing a report with the police (or through Lost112) isn't a legal requirement for reissuance, but it's commonly recommended so a found or stolen card can't be used to impersonate you.

## Step 2: Gather documents and book HiKorea

Reissuance can't be filed through HiKorea's online e-Application — you have to **book a visit and show up in person** at the immigration office with jurisdiction over your address, the same jurisdiction rule as first-time registration. Bring:

1. **The Integrated Immigration Application Form** (available at the office or on HiKorea in advance).

2. **Your passport**, original and a copy.

3. **One color ID photo**, 3.5 cm × 4.5 cm — required again if more than 6 months have passed since your last ARC photo was taken.

4. **Proof of your current address.**

5. **Your damaged card itself**, if that's the reason for reissuance. Skip this if your card was lost.

6. **Fee: 35,000 KRW**, the same amount as first-time issuance since the IC-chip fee increase took effect January 1, 2025.

## Step 3: At the immigration office, and what happens after

Processing for a reissued card runs on the same timeline as a first-time application: roughly **2 to 3 weeks**. You'll either pick the card up in person at the office where you applied or have it mailed, the same choice available for a first ARC.

If you need to prove your registration status while you're waiting — for a bank, a landlord, or a new phone plan — ask the counter staff directly whether they can issue proof of your pending application. Don't assume one exists; the Seoul guidebook and MOFA page describe that kind of interim receipt for first-time registration, but I couldn't confirm the same document is issued for a reissuance, so ask rather than plan around it.

## The permanent residence card's 10-year wall

[Article 33 of the Immigration Act](https://www.law.go.kr/LSW//lsLawLinkInfo.do?lsJoLnkSeq=900211860&lsId=001707&chrClsCd=010202&print=print) sets the validity period for a permanent resident's registration card — called a 영주증 — at exactly **10 years**, and requires the holder to get it reissued before that period ends. This is easy to miss precisely because F-5 status itself never expires: there's no extension filing, no stay-period sticker, nothing pushing the date in front of you the way a visa renewal would. The card is the only thing with a deadline.

The same document list and 35,000 KRW fee above apply to a 10-year renewal. Several visa-consultancy guides suggest applying within 6 months of your card's printed expiry date, though I couldn't trace that specific lead time back to the statute itself — the law only requires reissuance "before expiration," without naming how early you're allowed to file. Treat 6 months out as a safe planning window, and confirm your own office's rules on [HiKorea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr/resv/ResvIntroR.pt?locale=en) or by calling 1345.

## When it goes wrong

**If you're past the deadline.** Go to your immigration office as soon as you realize it, rather than waiting for a better moment. A late reissuance is still better than an ARC that doesn't match your current passport or photo, and whatever fine applies for being late is smaller than the trouble an expired card causes when a bank or landlord asks to see it.

**If your F-5 card's 10-year date has already passed.** Your permanent status doesn't lapse, but don't treat that as a reason to delay — the card itself is what every bank, phone carrier, and landlord actually checks, and an expired one stops working as ID the moment the printed date passes.

**If your application is rejected for a missing document.** The office can turn away an incomplete application the same way it can for a first-time registration — bring a photocopy of your passport page and lease as backup, and if your card was damaged rather than lost, don't leave the damaged card at home; you need to hand it over.

**If the replacement card itself has an error.** Treat it the same way as any mistake on an official document: report it at the same office before you rely on it for anything else, rather than assuming the office will catch it later.
