Three guides on this site mention it in passing and none of them explain it: an "embassy certificate or an Apostille" for your driving license, documents "Apostille-certified, for convention countries" for a pension refund, a diploma or criminal record check that needs authenticating before an immigration office will look at it. If you've never dealt with international paperwork before, the word itself is the first obstacle — it isn't a Korean term, there's no single office that handles every case, and which path applies to you depends on which country issued the document in the first place.

## What an apostille actually replaces

Before 1961, getting one country's document recognized in another meant a chain of authentications: a notary, then your country's foreign ministry, then the destination country's embassy, each one stamping the last one's signature. The Hague Apostille Convention of 1961 replaced that chain with a single certificate — the apostille — for documents moving between member countries. One stamp from one authority in the issuing country, and the destination country has to accept it without any further embassy step.

Korea joined later than most people assume. The Hague Conference's own [status table for the Convention](https://www.hcch.net/en/instruments/conventions/status-table/?cid=41) lists Korea's accession as **October 25, 2006**, taking effect **July 14, 2007**. That's the line that matters: a document apostilled before July 2007 for use in Korea wasn't covered by the Convention here yet. The same table shows the English-speaking countries that send the most foreigners to Korea joined at very different times — the UK in **1965**, the US in **1981**, Australia in **1995**, and Canada only recently, with its accession entering into force on **January 11, 2024**.

## Which path applies depends on where the document comes from

**Your document was issued in your home country, for use in Korea.** If your home country is a Convention member — which covers essentially every country that sends large numbers of foreign workers, students, and spouses to Korea, including the US, UK, Canada, and Australia — you get the apostille from the competent authority *in your home country* before you ever involve a Korean office. In the US, that's typically the Secretary of State's office in the state that issued the document; in the UK, the FCDO; in Canada, Global Affairs Canada; in Australia, the Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade. Korea has to accept an apostille from any of those bodies without a Korean embassy stamp on top of it — that's the entire point of both countries being Convention members.

**Your home country is not a Convention member.** Then the old chain process still applies: notarize the document, get it authenticated by your own country's foreign ministry, and then get it confirmed by a Korean embassy or consulate in that country — what Korean sources call 영사확인 (consular confirmation). This isn't a theoretical process; Korean missions publish their own procedure pages for it. The [Korean Embassy in Dhaka's consular confirmation page](https://overseas.mofa.go.kr/bd-en/brd/m_23303/view.do?seq=1) lays out exactly this: an original document with the required government or notary seals, a completed application form, and a fee — that mission charges **USD 4 per copy**, though the fee and office hours are set mission by mission, so check your specific embassy's own page rather than assuming the Dhaka numbers apply to you.

**Your document was issued in Korea, for use abroad.** This is the reverse direction, and it runs through [apostille.go.kr](https://www.apostille.go.kr/index.do?language=en), a joint service of Korea's Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Ministry of Justice. The listed fee is **1,000 won** per request, confirmed on both the English and Korean versions of the same page. Processing time depends on when you file and how many documents you bring, all for in-person applicants with 10 or fewer documents: filings before 11:30 a.m. or between 1:00 and 2:30 p.m. are issued within **1 hour** on average; filings between 11:30 a.m. and 1:00 p.m. come out at 2:00 p.m. the same day; anything filed after 2:30 p.m. is ready at 9:30 the next business day. Filing by mail takes roughly **7 to 10 days**. For a short, fixed list of document types, there's also a free e-apostille issued entirely online, with no physical visit at all. The in-person office — the Service Support Center for Overseas Koreans — sits on the 15th floor of Twin Tree Tower A in Jongno-gu, Seoul, open 9:00 a.m. to 6:00 p.m. (9 hours a day), Monday through Friday.

## Where this actually comes up

Immigration and licensing offices don't ask for an apostille for its own sake — it shows up as a line item inside a bigger filing:

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Exchanging a foreign driving license for a Korean one — the Road Traffic Authority's document list asks for "an embassy certificate or an Apostille for your license," confirmed in my <a href="/driving-in-korea-foreigners-license-exchange/">driving license exchange guide</a></li>
  <li>Filing for a National Pension lump-sum refund by mail after you've already left Korea, which requires your documents "notarized in your country of residence and then authenticated by a Korean consulate (or Apostille-certified, for convention countries)" — see my <a href="/national-pension-korea-foreigners-lump-sum-refund/">pension refund guide</a></li>
  <li>A status change built on a foreign civil document, like a marriage certificate backing an F-6 filing — see my <a href="/change-visa-status-korea-foreigners/">visa status change guide</a> for the 30-day filing deadline that starts once you have the document in hand</li>
  <li>Education and criminal-background-check documents for certain work visas, which immigration and HR offices generally require to carry either an apostille or consular confirmation before they'll accept a foreign-issued copy</li>
</ul>

In every one of these, the apostille or consular-confirmation step happens *before* you walk into the Korean office — it's not something an immigration officer or examiner can do for you on the spot, and it's not something you can start once you're already standing at the counter.

The figures behind two of those examples are worth having on hand. The driving-license exchange itself costs **15,000 won** for a mobile IC license, and a foreign license alone only lets you drive in Korea for **1 year** from your entry date — both numbers come from my driving license guide, not this one. A marriage-triggered status change carries its own **100,000 won** fee, doubled to **200,000 won** if you're moving into F-5 permanent residency, confirmed in my visa status change guide. And if the document is backing a National Pension lump-sum refund mailed in from overseas, the underlying claim right lapses after **5 years** regardless of how long the authentication itself takes — see my pension refund guide for that clock.

## When it goes wrong

**You got an apostille when you needed consular confirmation, or the other way around.** This only happens one way: your home country has to actually be a Hague Convention member for an apostille to work in Korea. If it isn't, no apostille — however official it looks — substitutes for the embassy's own consular confirmation, and an office that catches the mismatch will simply get your application rejected rather than flagging it for you in advance. Check your country's status on the [Hague Conference's status table](https://www.hcch.net/en/instruments/conventions/status-table/?cid=41) before you order anything, not after an office rejects it.

**The embassy or foreign ministry step is taking longer than your deadline.** The consular-confirmation chain has more steps than a single apostille — notary, then foreign ministry, then embassy — and each one is a separate queue. If you're racing a fixed deadline like the 30-day window for a marriage-based status change, start this process the day you have the underlying document, not the week before you need to file.

**You decide to skip the authentication step and drive or file anyway.** Don't. On the driving-license side specifically, operating on an unrecognized or expired foreign license carries the same exposure as never exchanging it at all: a fine of up to **3,000,000 won** or up to **1 year** in prison for unlicensed driving, per the driving guide's own reading of the Road Traffic Act.

**Your document is in the wrong language for the receiving office.** An apostille or consular confirmation certifies a signature and seal — it says nothing about whether the Korean office can read the document itself. A certified translation is usually a separate requirement on top of the authentication step, not a substitute for it; confirm both requirements with the specific office before you travel anywhere to get either done.

**You need a document authenticated in the other direction — Korean-issued, for use abroad — and the 1,000-won in-person fee window has already closed for the day.** Mail is always available as a fallback, but budget the full 7–10 days rather than assuming it will match the same-day in-person turnaround.
