Most of our visa guides — [E-2](/e2-visa-korea-english-teacher-requirements/), [E-7](/e7-visa-korea-specialized-worker-requirements/), [D-10](/d10-job-seeking-visa-korea-foreigners/) — cover a visa tied to one employer and one job offer. H-1, Korea's working holiday visa, is built differently: it's a reciprocal deal between Korea and a specific list of partner countries, meant for young travelers who want to fund a longer trip with casual work rather than commit to one employer from day one. That flexibility is exactly why people get the details wrong — assuming their country qualifies when it doesn't, or assuming any job is fair game when several categories explicitly aren't. Here's what the agreement actually covers, who's eligible, and what trips people up.

## Who actually qualifies

H-1 only exists because Korea has signed a bilateral working holiday agreement with a specific country or region — there's no general "working holiday visa" open to any nationality. [The Working Holiday Info Center run by Korea's Overseas Koreans Agency](https://whic.mofa.go.kr/whic/nation/info.jsp) lists the full partner roster: Andorra, Argentina, Australia, Austria, Belgium, Brazil, Canada, Chile, Czech Republic, Denmark, France, Germany, Hong Kong, Hungary, Ireland, Israel, Italy, Japan, Latvia, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, New Zealand, Poland, Portugal, Spain, Sweden, Taiwan, and the United Kingdom (listed under its own Youth Mobility Scheme, or YMS) — **30 countries and regions in total.**

**The United States is not on that list.** There's no H-1 working holiday pathway for US passport holders at all — a point worth stating plainly, since this site's audience includes a lot of American readers who may assume every major country has one.

Age limits aren't uniform either. A dated notice from the same info center — ["Working Holiday (H-1) Visa Information by Country and Region"](https://whic.mofa.go.kr/board.do?action=view&boardConfigNo=35&boardNo=17013&menuNo=100) — gives a per-country breakdown, and the range is real:

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Most partner countries: 18 to 30 years old (Australia, Austria, Belgium, Czech Republic, France, Hungary, Israel, Italy, Netherlands, New Zealand, Poland, Spain, Sweden, and others on the same notice)</li>
  <li>Canada and Luxembourg: 18 to 35</li>
  <li>The UK (YMS): 18 to 35, with a 1- or 2-year option</li>
  <li>Ireland, Germany, Denmark, Argentina, Brazil, Latvia, Portugal, Taiwan: 18 to 34</li>
</ul>

Several countries also carry an annual quota on the same notice — Canada's is the largest at **12,000**, while Australia, Denmark, Germany and Sweden have none listed. Don't assume your own country's number from this list, though: check your specific country's row on the official notice or your home country's Korean consulate page before you plan around an age cutoff or a quota, since agreements get renegotiated — Canada's terms changed in 2024, widening both the age ceiling and the annual quota from the older rules.

## What it costs and how long you can stay

There's no single H-1 fee or duration either — both are set per bilateral agreement. Two confirmed examples from the same info center, to show the actual range rather than a guess:

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Canada: visa fee <strong>CAD 279.75</strong> plus CAD 85 for biometrics, stay of up to 2 years per entry, with up to two separate stays allowed (4 years total lifetime cap)</li>
  <li>Czech Republic: visa fee roughly <strong>2,500 CZK</strong> (about 180,000 KRW at typical exchange rates), maximum stay around 12 months, quota of 300 on a first-come, first-served basis</li>
</ul>

If you're applying from Japan, [the Korean Consulate-General in Osaka's own H-1 page](https://overseas.mofa.go.kr/jp-osaka-ko/brd/m_20923/view.do?seq=1320927) gives a useful template for what the paperwork looks like even if your country's exact numbers differ: application form, a passport valid 6 months or more, a photo, a round-trip air ticket or booking made within 3 months of applying, a bank statement showing enough funds for a 3-month stay (300,000 JPY on that specific page), a travel plan, and a school enrollment or graduation certificate. Expect something broadly similar — form, funds, itinerary, education proof — whichever country you're applying from, but confirm your own country's exact figures and document list with your nearest Korean consulate rather than assuming Canada's or Japan's numbers apply to you.

## What H-1 doesn't let you do

H-1 is built around "tourism with some casual work on the side," not open work authorization, and a Korean consulate's own guidance on the program spells out what's off-limits. [That consular notice](https://overseas.mofa.go.kr/jp-ko/brd/m_1151/view.do?seq=1164968) lists four categories:

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Occupations that require a specific professional license or qualification — doctor, lawyer, professor, pilot, language instructor, private tutor</li>
  <li>Jobs at adult-entertainment venues (유흥접객업소) — service staff, dancers, singers, musicians, acrobats</li>
  <li>Activities that don't match the program's purpose — news reporting, political activity</li>
  <li>A general caution against entering Korea and immediately working full-time, rather than genuinely combining work with travel, which the notice frames as against the spirit of the agreement even where it isn't a specific named violation</li>
</ul>

Notice what's missing from that list: the notice doesn't cite a specific Immigration Control Act article number for these restrictions, only "the agreement" and "domestic laws" generally. If you need a legal citation for a specific employer or a specific job, that's a question for HiKorea or the issuing consulate, not something this guide will invent.

The flip side: regular casual jobs — café and restaurant work, retail, seasonal farm work, tourism-adjacent gigs — are generally the point of the visa, not an exception to it. The language instructor restriction is worth a second look if you're a native English speaker thinking you can pick up hagwon hours on H-1 the way you would on [E-2](/e2-visa-korea-english-teacher-requirements/) — you can't; that's explicitly one of the barred categories above, not a gray area.

## Once you're actually in Korea

Whichever country you're coming from, the same rules this site has already confirmed for every other visa category apply to H-1 too, per [the info center's own post-arrival page](https://whic.mofa.go.kr/contents.do?contentsNo=51&menuNo=114):

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Register for your Residence Card (ARC) within <strong>90 days</strong> of entry — see our [full ARC guide](/how-to-get-arc-korea/) for the step-by-step process and document list</li>
  <li>ARC issuance fee: <strong>35,000 KRW</strong>, cash</li>
  <li>If your stay runs past 6 months, you're required to enroll in National Health Insurance — our [health insurance guide](/korea-national-health-insurance-foreigners-cost/) covers what that costs</li>
  <li>If you move apartments during your stay, report the new address within 15 days — see our [address change guide](/report-address-change-korea-arc-15-days/)</li>
</ul>

A housing contract document is also one of the items the same page lists for Foreign Resident Registration, so keep a copy of your lease or guesthouse booking confirmation handy for your HiKorea appointment, same as every other visa category.

## When it goes wrong

**Your passport's country isn't on the partner list, or a recruiter told you otherwise.** Don't take anyone's word for which countries qualify — the roster and age ranges above came directly from the Working Holiday Info Center's own notice, and it's the only place to double-check before you commit to a flight or a job offer built around H-1 eligibility you haven't personally verified.

**You took a job in one of the barred categories.** Stop immediately once you know the restriction applies — continuing after the fact only adds to the exposure. If immigration raises it during an unrelated filing, answer directly rather than hoping it goes unnoticed.

**Your stay period runs out and you haven't left or changed status.** H-1 doesn't get special treatment here: our [visa extension guide](/extend-stay-period-korea-visa-extension-hikorea/) confirms that overstaying without approval under Article 25 of the Immigration Control Act carries a penalty of **up to 3 years in prison or a fine of up to 30,000,000 KRW** under Article 94, item 17 of the same Act, on top of deportation and re-entry-ban risk. If you're genuinely going to run past your date, go to your immigration office before it passes, not after.

**You can't find your own country's exact age limit, fee, or quota in this guide.** That's deliberate — the numbers above are confirmed examples (Canada, Czech Republic, Japan), not a universal table, because the terms are genuinely different for each partner country. Check your own country's row on [the official notice](https://whic.mofa.go.kr/board.do?action=view&boardConfigNo=35&boardNo=17013&menuNo=100) or call **1345** before you plan around a number you read somewhere else, including this post.
