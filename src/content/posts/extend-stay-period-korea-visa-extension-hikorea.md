Your stay period in Korea is a date printed on a sticker in your passport, and the law that governs what happens next is short: apply for an extension before that date, or you're staying illegally the moment it passes. There's no grace period written into the statute. Here's what the application actually involves, what it costs, what trips people up, and what happens if you miss the date anyway.

## Extension, not a new visa

A 체류기간 연장허가 (extension of stay period) keeps your current status of residence — E-2 teacher, D-2 student, F-6 spouse, whatever you hold — and simply pushes the expiration date further out. [Article 25 of the Immigration Control Act](https://www.law.go.kr/LSW//lsLawLinkInfo.do?lsJoLnkSeq=1000869901&lsId=001707&chrClsCd=010202&print=print) states it plainly: a foreigner who wants to keep staying beyond their stay period must receive the Minister of Justice's extension permission before that period ends. That's a different process from a 체류자격 변경허가 (change of status), which is what you'd file if you were switching, say, from a D-4 language-school visa to an E-7 work visa — different form, different document list, and usually a different reason to file. Our [guide to changing status](/change-visa-status-korea-foreigners/) covers that process and its fees separately.

## When to actually apply — and the number that isn't fixed

The enforcement decree backing Article 25 only says you must submit your application, with the documents your visa category requires, to the immigration office covering your address before your current stay period ends — it doesn't write in a specific number of months you're allowed to apply early. In practice, how far ahead HiKorea's online system and in-person counters will actually accept your application varies by visa category, and the guides that cite a specific lead time don't agree with each other: some say up to 4 months before expiration, others cite 5 months, and at least one source I checked said 2 months. None of those point back to a single rule in the text I could verify, so treat them as rough and check your own remaining window on [HiKorea's e-application page](https://www.hikorea.go.kr) rather than planning around someone else's number. What is fixed: the deadline is your stay period's actual end date, full stop, and there's no benefit to waiting until the last week.

Processing itself isn't instant either. Government guidance on the [stay-extension service listing](https://www.gov.kr/mw/AA020InfoCappView.do?CappBizCD=12700000097) puts ordinary processing at around 15 days, stretching to as long as 60 days for cases that need extra review — which is one more reason not to file in the final days before your sticker expires.

## What it costs and what approval looks like

The fee is set in the Immigration Control Act's enforcement rules, not negotiated case by case: **60,000 KRW** for most stay-extension applications, dropping to **30,000 KRW** for F-6 marriage-migrant status, per the fee schedule explained on the Ministry of Government Legislation's [easylaw.go.kr guide to visa extension](https://easylaw.go.kr/CSP/CnpClsMain.laf?popMenu=ov&csmSeq=1703&ccfNo=1&cciNo=4&cnpClsNo=1). File through HiKorea's electronic application instead of in person and the same enforcement rule knocks **20%** off that fee for extension, status-change, and re-entry applications alike.

Once the Minister of Justice approves your application, the enforcement decree's own description of the outcome is almost anticlimactic: the immigration office stamps your passport with the extension permission seal and writes in the new expiration date, or attaches an extension sticker — the same stamp-and-sticker process you likely remember from your very first [ARC registration](/how-to-get-arc-korea/). Nothing else about your status changes; you're not re-registered, and you don't need a new ARC unless you separately changed your address or personal details.

## Visa caps you can't extend past

Extension isn't unlimited for every status. Two examples worth knowing if they apply to you, both confirmed on the same easylaw.go.kr guide: an **E-8** seasonal-worker visa cannot be extended past **8 months** of continuous stay in a single season, and an **H-2** working-visit visa tops out at **3 years**, extendable to **5 years** only with an employer's formal recommendation. Hit one of those caps and no amount of paperwork keeps you in that status — you'd need to leave and reapply, or change status entirely, well before you reach the ceiling.

## When it goes wrong: denied, late, or overstayed

**If your extension is denied.** The immigration office doesn't just send a rejection and leave the rest to you. Under the enforcement decree covering denial notices, the written refusal has to state a departure deadline, and that deadline can't be more than **14 days** after the date the notice was issued — though the Minister of Justice can set it to your already-approved expiration date instead, or skip naming a deadline at all if you're being allowed to stay under your existing status while something else is resolved. If you get a denial, read the notice closely for which of those applies, and ask the issuing office directly what your actual options are — appealing, reapplying with stronger documents, or arranging departure — before the window closes.

**If you're genuinely going to be late.** Go to your immigration office before the deadline passes, not after, even if you don't have everything ready. A documented attempt to resolve your status before expiration is a meaningfully different position than silence followed by an overstay.

**If you overstay without any extension approval at all.** This is the scenario the law treats seriously. [Article 94, item 17 of the Immigration Control Act](https://www.law.go.kr/LSW/lsLawLinkInfo.do?lsJoLnkSeq=900532005&chrClsCd=010202) sets the penalty for continuing to stay past your period without Article 25 approval at **up to 3 years in prison or a fine of up to 30,000,000 KRW**, on top of the deportation and re-entry-ban consequences that typically follow. In practice, a short, first-time overstay is far more often handled as an administrative fine assessed at the immigration office according to how many days you went over, rather than as a criminal prosecution — but I couldn't independently confirm a current fine amount for every length of overstay against a single official table, so don't assume a specific number. If you've already overstayed even by a few days, go to the immigration office yourself and ask what your case requires rather than guessing from a blog post, including this one.

  <li>Know your stay period's exact end date — it's the only deadline that's actually fixed in law</li>

  <li>File with time for 15–60 days of processing, not in the final week</li>
