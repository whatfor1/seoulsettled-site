"You have Korean heritage, so you qualify for F-4" is only half true. The F-4 (재외동포, "overseas Korean") visa runs on a lineage test written into a specific law, not a general sense of ancestry, and it comes with a list of jobs you still can't hold even after you get it. If you've got a Korean grandparent and a job offer that sounds too casual to need E-7's paperwork, this is the visa people point you toward — but the eligibility test and the restricted-job list trip up more applicants than the application form itself does.

This guide covers who actually qualifies under the Act on Immigration and Legal Status of Overseas Koreans, what the visa does and doesn't let you do for work, and the three recurring deadlines — registration, address changes, and extensions — that generate the most support-desk calls.

## Who actually qualifies

The statute defines "overseas Korean" (재외동포) in two tiers, and F-4 only goes to the second one if you clear it:

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Korean nationals currently living abroad with permanent residency or long-term residence status in that country (재외국민)</li>
  <li>Foreign nationals of Korean descent — people who held Korean nationality at some point, or whose parent or grandparent did, before acquiring foreign nationality (외국국적동포)</li>
</ul>

The second category is where "my great-grandfather was Korean" claims usually fail — the law counts parent and grandparent generations, not further back, and you need a document trail proving the lineage: a family relation certificate, a Korean family register entry for the ancestor, or equivalent civil records from before they lost Korean nationality. Immigration offices and consulates abroad both reject applications where the applicant can state the lineage but can't document it.

F-4 status is granted for **3 years** at a time under Article 10(1) of the Act, renewable, with no cap on total years of renewal once you're in the system.

## What F-4 does and doesn't let you do

F-4 gives broader work rights than most other non-work visas — you don't need a separate work permit for most jobs — but it isn't unrestricted. The Ministry of Justice maintains a periodically-revised notice listing occupations F-4 holders cannot take. The version in effect now, **법무부고시 제2026-65호** (effective February 12, 2026), replaced the prior 2023-187호 notice and lists:

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>29 occupations under "simple labor" restrictions (Attachment 1) — manufacturing line work, construction labor, and similar roles the government reserves for other visa categories</li>
  <li>8 occupations under "public interest/morals" restrictions (Attachment 2) — including adult-entertainment-adjacent service work</li>
</ul>

That's **37 restricted occupations total**. If your job offer falls outside both lists, F-4 generally covers it without a separate work-permit filing — this is the main reason employers prefer hiring overseas Koreans on F-4 over sponsoring E-7 or E-9, since there's no quota or salary-floor filing on the employer's side.

One exemption narrows the restriction: F-4 holders who establish residence in a Ministry-of-Justice-designated population-declining region can take jobs from the "simple labor" list (Attachment 1) and the public-interest list where it overlaps with regional labor shortages — but the adult-entertainment-type restrictions in Attachment 2 are not waived by regional residence. Check the current region list and your specific occupation against the notice before assuming the exemption applies to you; it's tied to where you're registered, not just where the job is located.

## Registering: the 90-day deadline and the fee

Once you enter Korea on F-4, you have **90 days** to register a domestic residence (국내거소신고) at an immigration office — the same registration window as the standard Alien Registration Card process covered in our [ARC guide](/how-to-get-arc-korea/), though the F-4 card itself is called a 국내거소신고증 rather than an ARC. You'll need your passport, a resident-registration-style application, and a passport photo (3.5x4.5cm).

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Issuance or reissuance fee: 35,000 won, in person only — there's no online filing option for this specific step</li>
  <li>Processing time: about 3 hours at the counter, per the application's own processing-time listing</li>
</ul>

That fee and timing come directly from the government's own civil-service listing for this filing, not a third-party estimate — worth noting because several visa-agency blogs still quote a 30,000 won figure left over from before a 2025 fee adjustment.

## Moving house: the 14-day report

If you change your registered address after you've already filed your domestic residence report, Article 6(2) of the Act requires you to report the change within **14 days** — tighter than the 15-day window that applies to general foreign residents' address changes, which our [address-change guide](/report-address-change-korea-arc-15-days/) covers in full. You can file at the immigration office or, since a 2016 amendment widened where you can file, at your local district or town office instead of only at an immigration office.

One conflicting claim worth flagging for anyone who's seen it elsewhere: some secondary sources describe a 2023 amendment that supposedly stretched this to 30 days. Checking the law's own amendment history directly turns up no such change — the only 2023 amendment (Law No. 19434, effective June 2023) added a legal basis for mobile versions of the residence card, nothing about the reporting deadline. Treat any "30 days" claim you see elsewhere as wrong until a dated primary source backs it up; 14 days is what's currently in force.

## Extending your stay

File the extension before your current 3-year period runs out. The fee differs by filing method:

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Online, through HiKorea's e-application: 56,000 won</li>
  <li>In person at the immigration office: 60,000 won</li>
  <li>Processing time: about 14 days for either method, though the office notes a visit backlog can push it longer</li>
</ul>

## When it goes wrong

**You missed the 90-day registration window.** You're not automatically deported, but you're out of compliance and exposed to a fine the same way any unregistered foreign resident is. Register as soon as you realize — the fine schedule for late registration isn't published as a clean table on any official source we could verify, and secondary sources disagree on the exact bracket amounts, so call the Immigration Contact Center at **1345** for your specific case rather than relying on a number from a forum post.

**Your lineage documents got rejected.** This is the single most common F-4 application failure. If a consulate or immigration office rejects your family relation certificate or equivalent record, ask specifically what document would satisfy the gap — usually it's a missing generation link between you and the ancestor who held Korean nationality, and apostilled civil records from the country where that generation lived can sometimes bridge it. There's no appeal process that skips the document requirement; you either produce the record or you don't qualify under this category.

**You took a restricted job without realizing it.** F-4 doesn't come with a work permit stamp that flags restricted occupations for you — the burden is on you and your employer to check the current notice. If you're already working in a job that turns out to be on the restricted list, stop and consult an immigration lawyer or call 1345 before the employer self-reports or an inspection catches it; voluntarily correcting course looks very different in your record than getting caught.

**Your card was lost or the information on it changed.** Reissuance uses the same 35,000 won fee and in-person process as initial issuance — there's no separate "replacement" fee schedule.
