You can’t get paid, pay rent or use most Korean apps without a Korean bank account, and the first branch visit is where plans fall apart: wrong document, wrong phone, a “restricted” account you didn’t ask for. Here’s how to open a bank account in Korea as a foreigner, in the right order, so you only go once.

## What you’ll be asked for

Bring your passport, your residence card (the ARC), and proof of why you need the account, such as an employment certificate. Go to a branch on a weekday before 4 p.m. — business hours run 9 a.m. to 4 p.m., Monday to Friday. Skip the proof-of-purpose document and the branch doesn’t turn you away; it opens a restricted account capped at 3,000,000 won a day instead. Ask for online banking in the same visit, since a second trip just for that is avoidable.

## Before you go: what to bring

**After you have your residence card.** The Invest Korea guide to opening a bank account (August 2024) lists three things: your [residence card, passport and a home-country ID](https://www.investkorea.org/file/ik-en/How%20Foreigners%20Can%20Open%20a%20Bank%20Account%20in%20Korea.pdf); a document showing the purpose of your transactions; and, for tax-treaty reporting, proof you’ve been resident 183 days or longer if you want to be treated as a Korean resident for tax purposes. The Seoul Metropolitan Government’s [banking page](https://english.seoul.go.kr/service/living/convenience/1-banking) simply says to present your registration certificate and passport, and the branch gives you a bankbook and cash card on the spot. The same page draws the resident/non-resident line at 6 months in Korea, which also affects your interest and withholding tax rate.

**Before you have your residence card.** It’s possible, but the guide says you also need proof of a Korean address, for example a certificate of employment stating your place of residence, a utility bill, or a hotel voucher. If you use a hotel voucher, check with the branch first. If you haven’t got your card yet, start with [how to get your ARC](/how-to-get-arc-korea/) — you have 90 days from entry to register for one, but the bank visit doesn’t have to wait that long.

**Proof of purpose.** Korean banks must ask why you want the account, under Korea’s law on preventing telecom-based financial fraud. For salary, the Invest Korea guide accepts a certificate of employment, a wage and income-tax withholding receipt, an employment contract, or a specification of wages received over the past 3 months. For part-time pay, a contract or wage statement from the employer. Skip it and you get a restricted account.

> **Jun’s tip:** Ask your HR team for a certificate of employment (재직증명서) before you walk into the bank. It covers purpose and, if it lists your address, proof of residence too.

## What a “restricted account” means

If a document is missing, the bank can open a restricted account instead of refusing you outright. According to the Invest Korea guide, the daily limits are 3,000,000 won at a branch counter, 1,000,000 won at ATMs and 1,000,000 won through apps and online banking. You can lift the limit later by bringing in proof of your transaction purpose — the guide doesn’t give a processing time, so ask the branch directly. Rent deposits and big transfers will bump into that ceiling fast, so bring the paperwork the first time if you can.

## Step by step

1. **Pick one bank.** Large banks such as KB Kookmin, Shinhan, Woori and Hana all take foreign customers. The Invest Korea guide lists branches with English consulting, including Hana’s Itaewon branch, and foreigner-only phone lines such as Woori’s 1599-2288 and Shinhan’s 1577-8380.

2. **Go on a weekday.** Banking hours are Monday to Friday, 9:00 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. according to the [Seoul city page](https://english.seoul.go.kr/service/living/convenience/1-banking). Aim for mid-morning, not lunch hour.

3. **Hand over documents and fill in the form.** Passport, residence card, proof of purpose, and your Korean address.

4. **Ask for a debit card and online banking at the same time.** It’s much faster than coming back.

5. **Check the daily limit on your account** and ask what document lifts it.

## Opening an account in the app

KB Kookmin Bank says resident foreigners aged 19 years or older can open an account and sign up for internet banking in its [KB Star Banking app](https://kbthink.com/saving-guide/foreigner-account.html) without ever having banked with KB before. You need:

- A phone registered in your own name with a Korean carrier. Foreign carriers and prepaid SIMs don’t work for the identity check.

- A residence card, a permanent-residence card (issued since May 2011) or an overseas-Korean domestic residence report card — a mobile ID works too. A foreign passport or a Korean driver’s license can’t be used here.

- An existing Korean bank account number in your name, from any bank, to verify your identity.

Check the ID’s expiry date before you start. An expired or surrendered card is rejected automatically, and so is one belonging to someone whose stay in Korea has already ended. The name must also match across the phone carrier, ID and bank, down to spacing — if it doesn’t, the app sign-up is rejected and you’re sent to a branch or your carrier to fix it. The same page says first-time account holders in Korea should visit a branch first and talk to a staff member. That’s why the sequence is usually branch first, app later.

> **Jun’s tip:** Check your name spelling on your residence card, then check it again on your phone plan. A single space difference can block an app sign-up.

## Foreigner-specific accounts and fees

KB’s Global Star account is aimed at foreigners. As of its May 29, 2026 update, the page lists a 50% discount on exchange and overseas-transfer fees when you trade at a branch counter, plus a 70% exchange-fee discount when you move money from this account into a foreign-currency deposit. Fee waivers on internet-banking transfers and ATM withdrawals kick in once you have a single salary transfer of 500,000 won or more in the previous month. Terms change, so ask the branch what’s current. Deposits are protected up to 100,000,000 won per person, principal and interest combined, according to the same page.

## Sending money abroad

The Seoul city page says you can send up to $50,000 out of the country at once (including the exchange fee) and that sending an amount up to your Korean income needs documents showing where the money came from — your employment contract and recent pay slips. Different banks charge different fees, so the [separate guide to sending money abroad](/send-money-abroad-korea-foreigners/) covers how to compare them.

## If something goes wrong

**You forgot the proof-of-purpose document.** The branch doesn’t send you away — it opens a restricted account instead, capped at 3,000,000 won a day at the counter and 1,000,000 won through ATMs or apps. Come back with the missing paperwork and ask the branch to lift the limit; neither source gives a processing time for that, so ask at the counter how long it’ll take in your case.

**Your name doesn’t match across your phone, ID and bank.** Even a single space difference gets the KB app sign-up rejected. Fix the spelling at your carrier or at a branch before trying again — it’s a same-day fix, not a new application.

**You applied for a credit card on the same visit and it was rejected.** Seoul’s own banking guide warns that a thin Korean credit history can get a credit-card request turned down. A debit card tied to the account you just opened works immediately and doesn’t need a credit check — ask for it in the same visit.

**You don’t have your ARC yet.** You can still open an account on a passport plus proof of a Korean address, but if that proof is a hotel voucher, check with the branch before you go — some won’t accept it, and you don’t want to find that out after a wasted trip.

## Checklist

  <li>Passport and residence card (valid, not expired)</li>
  <li>Certificate of employment or other proof of purpose</li>
  <li>Proof of Korean address if you don’t have the card yet</li>
  <li>Korean phone number in your own name, matching ID spelling exactly</li>
  <li>Time before 4 p.m. on a weekday</li>
