Being enrolled on a D-2 (degree) or D-4 (language program) visa doesn't give you the right to take a part-time job the moment you land. That right belongs to a separate approval — 시간제 취업허가, a part-time work permit — and working without it falls under the same unauthorized-activity rule that applies to side income on a work visa. This guide covers what the permit actually requires, what it costs, and the specific things that put it at risk once you have it.

## The permit sits on top of your student status, not inside it

[Article 20 of the Immigration Control Act](https://www.law.go.kr/LSW/lsLawLinkInfo.do?lsJoLnkSeq=1000822779&chrClsCd=010202) requires anyone doing anything outside what their current status covers to get a 체류자격 외 활동허가 — permission for activity outside status — first. D-2 and D-4 status covers study, not employment, so a part-time job needs this permission regardless of how few hours it is or how informal the employer is about it.

[The gov.kr civil-service listing for this specific permit](https://www.gov.kr/portal/service/serviceInfo/PTR000050524) confirms the application fee is **면제** — exempted, no charge — and states processing time as **14일 이내**, within 14 days, though it typically clears faster than that in practice. That's unusually fast for an immigration filing — the status-change and residency applications our other guides cover often run 3 weeks to 3 months, not a fixed 14-day window. The legal basis is [Article 29 of the Immigration Control Act's Enforcement Regulations](https://www.gov.kr/portal/service/serviceInfo/PTR000050524), and the one document gov.kr specifies beyond the standard application form is an 외국인 유학생 시간제 취업 추천서 — a part-time work recommendation letter — submitted as a JPG file no larger than 450KB. That file-size cap matters more than it sounds: a phone photo of a signed recommendation letter often exceeds it, and a rejected upload resets your submission.

## Two employers at a time, one year at a time

The permit doesn't cover unlimited work arrangements once granted. gov.kr's listing caps the approval at **최장 1년** — up to 1 year — meaning it needs renewing, not a one-time approval for the rest of your program. University international-student offices that administer the recommendation-letter step (Yonsei's Global Lounge among them) describe the practical limit as **two** work locations at once — you can't hold approvals at three different part-time jobs simultaneously even if your weekly hours would technically allow it.

## Why this guide won't print a weekly-hour table

Every source that discusses D-2 and D-4 part-time work agrees the weekly hour cap scales with your program level and your Korean-language proficiency — a first-year bachelor's student without a TOPIK score gets fewer hours than a graduate student who clears the language bar, and most degree students get unlimited hours during school breaks if they meet the language requirement. Where sources disagree is the exact bracket: one labor-law firm's published summary puts the flat ceiling at 20 hours a week for undergraduates and 30 hours a week for graduate students, with a holiday exception only for degree students; a separate guide citing a HiKorea manual update breaks it into finer tiers — as low as 10 hours for students who haven't met the language bar, a middle tier around 25 hours for many bachelor's students, and up to 30 hours or 35 hours for those in preferential categories. Neither of those is a government page I could fetch and read directly; the only numbers in this guide that are are the fee, processing time, and permit duration above.

Rather than print a bracket table that two sources describe differently, the honest version is: your specific weekly cap depends on your degree level, your year in the program, and whichever Korean-proficiency tier you clear, and the only way to get the number that applies to you is to check it directly — through [HiKorea's](https://www.hikorea.go.kr) status-by-status manual, your school's international office (the same office that issues the recommendation letter), or a call to **1345**, the foreigner information hotline. Don't plan a work schedule around a number from a blog, including this one, when the bracket tables themselves don't agree.

## Grades and attendance keep the permit, not just get it

Getting approved the first time isn't the only academic bar. gov.kr's listing states that a renewal can be refused if your **attendance falls below 70%** or your **average grade in the most recent semester is C+ or lower**, on the reasoning that combining work and study is no longer judged workable at that point. Several university international-office guides and labor-law summaries separately describe the initial eligibility bar as a prior-semester GPA average of **C (2.0)** or higher for D-2 applicants, and roughly **90%** attendance for D-4 language-track applicants — those two figures aren't on the gov.kr listing itself, so treat them as the commonly cited standard rather than a number this guide independently confirmed on a government page, and verify your own transcript against your school's specific cutoff before you plan a part-time schedule around it.

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Get the part-time work permit approved before your first shift, not after — Article 20 doesn't have a grace period for applications already submitted</li>
  <li>Keep the recommendation-letter photo under 450KB and in JPG format, or the upload gets rejected and your filing date resets</li>
  <li>Track your own attendance and grades against your school's cutoff each semester — a renewal denial isn't appealable the way a late filing is</li>
  <li>Confirm your own weekly-hour bracket through HiKorea or 1345 rather than a secondhand table, since published summaries disagree on the exact numbers</li>
</ul>

## What it costs to get this wrong

[Article 94, item 12 of the Immigration Control Act](https://www.law.go.kr/LSW/lsLinkProc.do?lsNm=%EC%B6%9C%EC%9E%85%EA%B5%AD%EA%B4%80%EB%A6%AC%EB%B2%95&joNo=009400&mode=11) sets the penalty for unauthorized activity outside status — working without this permit falls squarely under that category — at **up to 3 years in prison or a 30,000,000 won fine**, the same ceiling our freelance income tax guide cites for work-visa holders doing undisclosed side work. Immigration enforcement doesn't need to catch you mid-shift to apply this; it comes up during unrelated filings too, including a status change or a stay extension review.

## When it goes wrong

**You started working before the permit cleared.** Stop the unapproved work immediately and get the permit filed — the exposure under Article 94 doesn't reset by applying late, but every additional shift under an unapproved status adds to it. If an immigration officer raises it during an unrelated review — a [stay extension](/extend-stay-period-korea-visa-extension-hikorea/), for instance — answer directly rather than guessing what they already know.

**Your attendance or grades dropped below the renewal threshold.** A denied renewal isn't the same as a denied extension of your underlying D-2 or D-4 status — your enrollment can still be fine while the work permit specifically lapses. Talk to your international-student office before the semester ends if attendance or grades are close to the line; some programs have a documented-reason exception (illness, a family emergency) that a straight cutoff reading doesn't show.

**Your recommendation-letter upload keeps getting rejected.** Check the file format and size first — JPG, 450KB maximum — before assuming it's a content problem with the letter itself. A format rejection and a substantive rejection look identical from the applicant's side but have completely different fixes.

**You're not sure which weekly-hour bracket applies to you.** Don't average two different blog tables and split the difference. Call **1345** or ask your school's international office, which deals with your specific university's recommendation-letter process and will know the current HiKorea standard it's filed against.
