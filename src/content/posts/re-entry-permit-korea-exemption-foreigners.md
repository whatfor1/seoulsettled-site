If you registered as a foreigner in Korea and you're planning a trip home, the question that used to send people down a HiKorea rabbit hole was: do I need a 재입국허가 (re-entry permit) before I leave, or does my alien registration just... stop existing while I'm gone? For most people today, the honest answer is you don't need one at all. That wasn't true a few years ago, and the old answer is still floating around in outdated guides. Here's the current rule, who it doesn't cover, and what happens if you get the deadline wrong either way.

## The short version: most trips don't need a permit anymore

Korea suspended its blanket re-entry-permit exemption during the pandemic, then restored it. [A Ministry of Foreign Affairs overseas-mission notice](https://overseas.mofa.go.kr/gr-ko/brd/m_6995/view.do?seq=1345418) states the exemption was resumed effective **April 1, 2022**, and it has stayed in effect since. Under that notice, if you hold a registered status in the broad A-1/A-3-through-H-2 range (which covers the large majority of work, study, marriage-migrant, and working-visit statuses) you can leave Korea and come back within **1 year** of your departure date — or within whatever's left of your stay period, if that's shorter — without applying for any permit at all. [F-5 permanent residents](/f5-permanent-residency-korea-foreigners/) get a **2-year** window instead of 1. F-4 overseas-Korean status holders are exempt for as long as their stay period runs. Refugee travel document holders are exempt until their document's own expiry date.

This is a genuinely different rule from what you'll find in older blog posts and even in some immigration-service PDFs still online, which describe a stricter, pandemic-era version of this system — mandatory permits for departing registered foreigners, a 72-hour pre-departure test requirement, consent forms dated 2021. That version was suspended and then superseded by the 2022.4.1 resumption notice above. If a guide you're reading doesn't mention the 1-year exemption window by name, assume it's describing the old rule.

## Who still needs to actually apply

The exemption has edges, and three groups fall outside it: anyone staying abroad longer than their exemption window (more than 1 year for most statuses, more than 2 for F-5), anyone whose status isn't in the exempted range, and anyone who wants the flexibility of multiple trips spaced out over a longer period than the automatic window covers. For those cases, [Article 30 of the Immigration Control Act](https://www.law.go.kr/LSW/lsLawLinkInfo.do?chrClsCd=010202&lsJoLnkSeq=1000817401) is the legal basis for applying, and the fee schedule sits in the enforcement rule attached to that article:

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>단수재입국허가 (single re-entry, one trip): 30,000 won, or 24,000 won filed online (20% off)</li>
  <li>복수재입국허가 (multiple re-entry, more than one trip within the permit's validity): 50,000 won, or 40,000 won filed online</li>
  <li>Processing time: 3 days</li>
  <li>Document needed: your passport, if you're applying in person — online filing skips the in-person document step</li>
  <li>Form: 서식 제34호, the same unified application form used for extension and status-change filings</li>
</ul>

That 20% online discount is the same enforcement-rule discount our [extension guide](/extend-stay-period-korea-visa-extension-hikorea/) and [status-change guide](/change-visa-status-korea-foreigners/) already cite — it applies across all three filing types, not just this one. File through HiKorea's electronic system rather than walking into a branch and you save the same fraction every time.

The 3-day turnaround is fast compared to the other two filings in this series: extension processing runs 15 days to 60 days, and status change runs 3 weeks to 3 months, because both involve a substantive review of your new activity or circumstances. A re-entry permit doesn't — the office is just checking your passport and your current status, which is why it clears in a fraction of the time.

## The actual risk: missing the return deadline, not forgetting a form

Here's the part that matters more than the paperwork. [Article 37-2 of the Immigration Control Act](https://www.law.go.kr/LSW/lsLinkProc.do?lsNm=%EC%B6%9C%EC%9E%85%EA%B5%AD%EA%B4%80%EB%A6%AC%EB%B2%95&joLnkStr=%EB%B2%95+%EC%A0%9C37%EC%A1%B0%EC%9D%982%EC%A0%9C2%ED%95%AD&ordinNm=%EC%B6%9C%EC%9E%85%EA%B5%AD%EA%B4%80%EB%A6%AC%EB%B2%95&lsId=005256&lsClsCd=OL&joNo=003702000&ancYd=20170816&mode=10&chrClsCd=010202) lets an immigration office cancel (말소) your alien registration if you don't make it back into Korea within your re-entry permit's period — or, if you were exempt from needing a permit, within the exemption period or your stay-permission period. In other words, the 1-year (or 2-year) exemption window isn't just a courtesy that lets you skip a form. It's a hard deadline with the same consequence as blowing a permit: come back late, and your registration can be struck off, meaning you'd need a fresh visa to re-enter rather than just walking back in on your old status.

This is why the exemption window matters more than the fee table for most readers. A fee you can pay in 3 days of processing. A cancelled registration means starting your immigration paperwork over from a visa application, not from a HiKorea counter.

## When it goes wrong

**You're going to be abroad longer than your exemption window allows.** Apply for a re-entry permit, ideally online, before you leave — not from abroad. The 3-day processing time means this is realistic to do even if your trip firms up at the last minute, but it still has to happen before departure; there's no process for requesting one once you're already outside Korea.

**You assumed you were exempt and you weren't.** If your status isn't in the A-1/A-3 through H-2, F-4, or F-5 range the MOFA notice covers, don't guess — call 외국인종합안내센터 at **1345** or ask your immigration office directly before you book flights. Getting this wrong doesn't just cost a fee; it risks the Article 37-2 cancellation above if you're out of the country when the mistake surfaces.

**If you miss the return deadline while you're already abroad.** If you realize you'll exceed your exemption window or permit period before you can get back, contact your nearest Korean embassy or consulate, or your immigration office in Korea directly, before the deadline passes rather than after. A documented attempt to resolve this before the cutoff is a materially different position than silence followed by a cancelled registration you only discover at the airport.

**If your application is denied or sent back.** Because the review only checks your passport and your current registered status, a rejection here is almost always a document or eligibility mismatch rather than a judgment call — the office will tell you on the spot what's missing, and most applicants can fix it and refile well within the same 3-day processing window rather than starting over.

**Your status changed while you were counting on the old exemption.** The 1-year/2-year windows run from your departure date on the status you held when you left. If your status changes mid-trip in some unusual case, or you're not sure which window applies to you, this is exactly the kind of edge case to confirm at 1345 rather than assume from a general guide like this one.
