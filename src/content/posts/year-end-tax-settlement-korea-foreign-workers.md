Every January, Korean payroll departments run a calculation called 연말정산 (year-end tax settlement): it compares the income tax your employer withheld from your paychecks all year against what you actually owed, and pays out or claws back the difference in your February salary. Foreign employees go through the same process as Korean coworkers, with one extra option most Koreans don't get: a flat tax rate that trades away every deduction for a single flat percentage. Here is how the settlement works, when it happens, who the flat rate actually helps, and what to do if your employer gets it wrong or you miss it.

## What the settlement actually reconciles

Your monthly paycheck already has income tax withheld, based on a simplified table tied to your salary and declared number of dependents. That withholding is almost never exactly right, because it doesn't know about your actual deductions — insurance premiums, medical bills, credit card spending, rent, dependents you support. 연말정산 is the correction: your employer collects your deduction evidence, recalculates your real tax liability for the year, and compares it to what was already withheld.

Whether you get the full range of deductions depends on residency, not nationality. If you've stayed in Korea 183 days or more in the tax year, you're a resident for tax purposes and get the same deduction menu as a Korean employee. Stay under that line and you're a non-resident, limited mostly to basic deductions tied directly to your Korean-sourced income.

## The calendar: when this actually happens

The National Tax Service runs this on a fixed yearly rhythm, confirmed each December for the coming season. For the settlement covering 2025 income, filed in early 2026, the [official schedule](https://www.korea.kr/news/policyNewsView.do?newsId=148958235) ran like this: companies registered their employee lists on Hometax by January 10, the simplified deduction-data service opened on January 15, corrected or late data could still be added through January 18, and the final confirmed dataset was ready January 20. Employers then finalize the numbers and reflect the result in that month's payroll.

Expect the same general shape — simplified service opening in mid-January, employer submission in the following days, result landing in your paycheck before the end of February — for the settlement covering 2026 income. The exact dates for that cycle aren't published yet; the NTS announces them every December, so check Hometax or ask your HR team once the new year starts rather than relying on last year's calendar.

## The 19% flat tax, and who it actually helps

Korea's ordinary income tax runs on a progressive scale from 6% up to 45% depending on your taxable income bracket. Foreign workers get an alternative under [조세특례제한법 제18조의2](https://www.law.go.kr/LSW/lsLawLinkInfo.do?lsJoLnkSeq=1001022464&lsId=001584&chrClsCd=010202&print=print): a flat 19% national tax rate on your gross wage income, with no deductions, exemptions, or tax credits applied at all. On top of that 19%, a local income tax surcharge equal to 10% of the national tax amount still applies, for a combined effective rate just under 21%.

You can only elect the flat rate for income earned within 20 years of the date you first provided labor in Korea, and only if that first work date fell on or before December 31, 2026 — a sunset written directly into the law. Executives or employees who hold a 30% or greater stake in the company that employs them, or who are otherwise classified as a related party under the law's implementing decree, are excluded from the option entirely.

The trade-off is simple math: the flat rate helps high earners with few deductions to claim. If your real deductions (housing, dependents, insurance, medical) would push your effective progressive rate below 19%, taking the ordinary route and claiming them wins instead. Run both numbers before you sign the election form — your employer's payroll system or an accountant can calculate both sides in minutes.

A heads-up for next year: the government's 2026 tax reform proposal would raise this flat rate from 19% to 21% and extend the sunset date to December 31, 2029, to narrow the gap with what Korean employees pay. As of this writing that's a legislative proposal, not yet law — the current 19% rate is what applies to 2026 income.

## Deductions foreign workers often skip because they assume they don't qualify

Two benefits changed for 2026, and both came up repeatedly in official coverage of foreign-worker settlements this year, per [Korea.net's report on the changes](https://www.korean-culture.org/koreanet/view.do?seq=1053528):

- **Housing fund savings deduction, now open to spouses.** Previously only the household head could claim the 주택마련저축 (housing-fund savings) income deduction. Starting with 2025 contributions, the household head's spouse can also claim it, worth 40% of what was paid into the qualifying savings product, up to 3,000,000 won a year, provided total salary is 70,000,000 won or less and the household owns no home.
- **Foreign engineers: a 50% income tax reduction for 10 years.** Foreign workers who transfer technology under a registered engineering contract, or who hold a science/engineering degree plus qualifying overseas R&D experience, get half their earned income tax waived for 10 years from their start date.

Renters often miss the monthly rent tax credit entirely, assuming it's a homeowner-only system. Under [조세특례제한법 제95조의2](https://www.law.go.kr/LSW/lsLawLinkInfo.do?chrClsCd=010202&lsJoLnkSeq=1000027473), if you're a non-homeowning household head (or a family member of one, as long as nobody in the household owns a home), paying rent on a unit at or under Korea's 85㎡ national housing scale (100㎡ in eup or myeon areas outside the metropolitan region, per the [Housing Act](https://www.law.go.kr/LSW//lsLawLinkInfo.do?lsJoLnkSeq=900126958&lsId=001809&chrClsCd=010202&print=print)) with an officially assessed value of 400,000,000 won or less, you can claim a credit of 15% of your annual rent if your total salary is 80,000,000 won or less, rising to 17% if your total salary is 55,000,000 won or less. The credited rent amount is capped at 10,000,000 won a year, so the maximum possible credit is 1,700,000 won. If you're renting under a [jeonse or wolse arrangement](/jeonse-wolse-renting-apartment-korea-foreigners/), check your contract's deposit-to-rent split against these numbers before assuming you don't qualify.

## When it goes wrong: missed deadlines, wrong documents, and denied credits

Mistakes happen on both sides, and none of them are final. If you missed the January submission window entirely, if your employer's settlement has a wrong document on file, or if a credit you claimed was denied because the paperwork didn't match, you still have a path back to the right number. Under [국세기본법 제45조의2](https://www.law.go.kr/LSW/lsLawLinkInfo.do?lsJoLnkSeq=900034551&chrClsCd=010202), you can file a 경정청구 (request for correction) within 5 years of the statutory filing deadline for the tax year in question — well past the deadline the employer's settlement runs on. File it through Hometax (신고/납부 → 세금신고 → 종합소득세 → 근로소득자 신고서 → 경정청구) or in person at your district tax office, and the tax authority has to process a refund within about 2 months of receiving the request.

This also covers gaps your employer's settlement never caught: a dependent you forgot to list, a medical bill you paid out of pocket, a rent credit you didn't know existed. If a correction request is rejected because the tax office disagrees with your documentation, ask for the specific reason in writing — you can refile with better evidence inside the same 5-year window rather than treating one rejection as final. If you earned taxable income outside your main job that your employer's settlement couldn't account for, that usually needs a separate 종합소득세 (comprehensive income tax) filing in May rather than a correction request, so check with your tax office which path applies if your situation involves outside income.

<ul class="checklist">
  <li>Confirm you're a tax resident: 183 days or more in Korea during the year</li>
  <li>Check whether the 19% flat rate or the ordinary progressive rate costs less for you</li>
  <li>Gather rent, insurance, medical and dependent records before the simplified service opens in January</li>
  <li>Ask HR whether your household qualifies for the rent credit or housing savings deduction</li>
  <li>Have your ARC number ready — Hometax and payroll systems both key off it</li>
  <li>If something was missed, file a 경정청구 within 5 years rather than writing off the refund</li>
</ul>
